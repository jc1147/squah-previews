Section 1

What their inventory is

Their sitemap — the index file a site gives search engines listing every page it wants found — holds 3,868 URLs. Of those, 1,227 are individual licence listings. We fetched every one of them at no more than two requests a second, and parsed each with a single extractor built against the real page structure. All 1,227 returned HTTP 200. That makes the numbers in this section a census — a complete count of the population, not an estimate drawn from a sample.

Section 2

The “fake listings” question

This engagement began with a claim worth testing properly: that the competitor’s inventory is fabricated. We ran ten separate tests across the full census to find out. The answer has two halves, and the second half matters as much as the first.

The blanket claim is contradicted. census, n = 1,227

Measured across all 1,227 listing pages, the claim that the inventory as a whole is fake does not survive. Four independent measurements point away from it.

Three narrower criticisms do hold, and they travel with the verdict: a measurable minority of the records are junk; the displayed “Views” counter is not a real measurement; and the authenticity of any single listing is undetermined, because no listing publishes a key anyone could check against a public register.

What points away from fabrication

  • The stock turns over. Of 385 listing URLs the web archive recorded as live and that have since left the sitemap, 220 now return “not found”. A matched control of 40 URLs still in the sitemap, fetched the same way, all returned normally. Pages get deleted — which a site built purely to attract search traffic has no reason to do.
  • The prices are not a generated default. 271 distinct price values across 1,227 listings; 165 of them are not round thousands. A machine-filled field usually shows a small value set or a fixed step.
  • The central price distribution is economically coherent. Excluding 25 impossible rows: n = 1,202, quarter points $25,000 and $175,000, median $75,000.
  • Every page is backed by its own database record. The internal product code equals “auction-<id>” on 1,227 of 1,227 pages, with no duplicates.
  • The seller intake is serious. Their “sell your licence” form has 17 required fields, including a state licence number, the licensee name exactly as it appears on the licence, and the premises address.
  • The county spread is market-shaped. Heavily concentrated in large metros (Gini 0.6105), with 62 counties holding exactly one listing — not the flat count a per-county generator would produce.

What is genuinely wrong with it

  • 63 records are placeholders or junk. 25 carry prices that cannot be a licence transfer — three at an all-nines maximum value, others at $4, $5, $10 — and 42 carry an empty or malformed county or licence type. Four records fall into both groups, so the distinct total is 63. All of them are live, marked available, and listed in the sitemap given to search engines.
  • The “Views” counter is not a measurement. It is bounded to 0–36 with every integer in that range present, near-flat in frequency, and carries no relationship to listing age. One listing reports more bids than views, which is impossible for a real counter. Archived pages show the same counter falling over time and once reaching 96 — a cumulative count cannot decrease.
  • 37 pairs of consecutive listing IDs hold identical records. Same state, county, type and price, 3.02% of listings — the signature of rows inserted together rather than sellers arriving separately.
  • Nothing is externally checkable. Zero of 1,227 listings publish a licence number, licensee name or premises address, so no buyer or competitor can verify a single one against the state register.
  • The order book is nearly empty. 1,199 of 1,227 listings show zero bids. Twenty-eight have ever taken one; the maximum is seven.
  • Two template defects sit on every page. All 1,227 page titles render “$0k” while the page body shows the true price, and all 1,227 leak raw server code into their structured data, which makes that markup unreadable to search engines.

Why the view counter reads as generated

This is the sharpest single finding in the census, so here is the actual shape. A real lifetime view count is unbounded, heavily skewed — a few pages get a lot, most get very few — and grows with age. The measured counter does none of that.

Two supporting statistics: the coefficient of variation — how much the bar heights vary relative to their average — is 0.346, where organic web traffic would be well above 1. And the correlation between listing ID and view count is 0.021, essentially none, where an older listing should have accumulated more views and therefore show a negative relationship. Stated fairly

A decorative or periodically reset counter on a genuine listing is an alternative explanation for a bounded number, and the data cannot rule it out. What the data does establish is narrower and still important: the counter cannot be read as a record of real traffic, and therefore no argument about the inventory being real or fake can rest on those counters varying — including an earlier argument in this engagement that did, which has been withdrawn.

What cannot be settled from outside

Authenticity of any individual listing is undetermined for 100% of the census, and that is a limitation of what the competitor publishes rather than a finding about them. The California register is searchable by licence number, licensee name or premises address. None of those three appears on any listing page, so ten individual cross-check attempts all returned “undetermined for lack of a key” — not “no match found”.

One aggregate test was attempted and is reported as inconclusive rather than as evidence: whether their California off-sale beer-and-wine listings sit in counties where the state has frozen new issuance of that class. They measured 41.6% in frozen counties — but the freeze applies mostly to small rural counties while listings concentrate in big metros, so both a real and a generated inventory would produce the same answer. The test has no power to distinguish them and was discarded. The authoritative per-county licence-count report that would have fixed this sits behind a path the state’s own robots file asks crawlers not to fetch, so it was not fetched.

Six images for 1,227 pages

Across the entire listing census there are six distinct images: five state banners (California on 734 pages, New Jersey 131, Florida 102, Arizona 63, Michigan 19) and one email-campaign logo on the remaining 178. A separate 16-page sample of listing pages found that none of the sixteen carried a single image unique to that page. How much of a listing page is actually about the listing

A 16-page sample measured the split between boilerplate and unique text. sample, n = 16 A median listing page ran 1,097.5 words, of which 675.5 were outside the site’s repeated furniture — navigation, footer and the standard fee tables. But “outside the furniture” is not the same as “about this licence”: the census puts the licence-specific description at 78 words. The remainder is county and state guidance shared with every sibling listing in the same group.

The same sample measured text overlap between pairs of listing pages using an 8-word-run comparison — the standard way to detect near-duplicate writing. The result is split in two: of 120 page pairs, 74 share almost nothing, while 46 overlap heavily and 11 of those are effectively identical. That is exactly what a template keyed to state and type produces: pages in different groups look different, pages in the same group are near copies.

One measurement disagreement, stated rather than smoothed over

Two runs measured whole-page word count with different text extractors and disagree: the census reports a median of 1,374 words per listing page (n = 1,227), the page-anatomy run 1,097.5 (n = 16). The anatomy run documents why — a naive tag-stripper leaks embedded script attributes into the text and inflated one of these very pages by about 25% in testing — so the lower figure is probably the better one. Neither figure changes the finding that matters, which is the 78-word licence-specific description measured on the full census.

Section 4

What they publish, and what they hold back

Read as a list this looks damning, and it should not. Most of what they withhold is what a broker is supposed to withhold. A premises street address or a licensee’s legal name identifies the business to its own staff, customers and competitors before a sale closes, and not publishing it is the ordinary convention of business brokerage rather than a defect. We separate the two kinds of omission below.

Public on every listing page

  • Asking price — 1,227 of 1,227 (100%)
  • State — 1,227 (100%)
  • Licence type — 1,227 (100%)
  • County — 1,206 (98.3%)
  • A description — 1,198 (97.6%)
  • An image — 1,227 (100%), drawn from six files
  • An availability status of “in stock” — 1,227 (100%)
  • Bid count and view count — 1,227 (100%)
  • A full, itemised fee schedule — auction fee, escrow fee, state filing fees, with the escrow company and its regulator named. Escrow is discussed on 878 listings (71.6%).

Never published on any listing page

  • Licence number — 0 of 1,227
  • Licensee or individual seller name — 0 of 1,227. The only seller named in the page markup is the marketplace itself, on all 1,227.
  • Premises address, or even a city — 0 on the listing page. A city does appear on their index rows.
  • Any date — 0 in the page markup; 19 visible, and those are one statutory date inside licence copy, not listing dates.
  • Any status other than available — nothing is ever shown as sold, pending or in escrow.
  • Licence conditions or transfer history — no field for it exists on any page.
  • A way to ask a question without an account — every offer and bid button on all 1,227 pages resolves to a single login URL, and their registration form has 19 required fields.

Is the missing licence number deliberate?

Unknown, and we will not guess. The measurement is that no listing carries one. A deliberate confidentiality policy and a database that never had the field are equally consistent with what we can see from outside, and this should not be described as concealment. It is worth noting the two facts side by side: their seller intake form requires a state licence number, the licensee name exactly as it appears on the licence, and the premises address — 17 required fields in all — so the data reaches them. None of those three is then published.

The transaction is gated; the content is not

This distinction is worth being precise about, because it is the design decision underneath their whole site. Price, county, type, description, bid count and view count all render to an anonymous visitor who is not logged in — no wall, no email capture, nothing hidden. What is gated is acting: all 1,227 offer and bid buttons point to the same login page. Their sell, register and login pages are themselves fully open to search engines with no exclusion tags, and their robots file — the file that tells crawlers where they may go — blocks nothing at all: zero of their 3,868 sitemap URLs are disallowed to a general crawler, and 17 named AI crawlers are each explicitly allowed the whole site.

Section 5

Their dates, and the absence of them

Across the full census, no listing declares or displays a listing date. Not a posted date, not an updated date, not days-on-market, not “new” or “price reduced”. In the page markup, zero of 1,227 carry a publish date, a modified date or a price-valid-until date. Nineteen listings show a visible date and all nineteen are the same string — a statutory cut-off date of 1 April 1988 sitting inside Michigan licence copy. It is not a listing date and we do not count it as one.

A buyer on any of those 1,227 pages therefore cannot tell a licence posted last week from one posted in 2020. That is a conversion and trust finding. It is not evidence that the inventory never moves — movement is separately proven by the 220 removed URLs described in Section 2. Stock does move; it moves invisibly.

What their sitemap claims about freshness

Every one of their 3,868 URLs carries a “last modified” date. For the listing pages, all 1,227 of those dates fall inside the five days before our pull, with near-unique timestamps, marked “changes daily”. Read literally, that claims the entire inventory changed that week. Nothing in the census corroborates it: of the 26 listings whose price could be recovered from an archived copy, none differed from the live price (one had changed between two archived snapshots), 132 description texts still cover 1,198 listings, and the touches run through all 24 hours of the clock including overnight. This associates with an automated row-touch — a scheduled job stamping records — rather than with editorial change. Stated as an association; the cause is not measured.

The contrast with their content pages is stark, and to their credit it runs the honest way round: the pages that carry their traffic carry old, unrestamped dates.

What their pages used to look like

Fourteen years of archived homepages tell a consistent story: this has been presented as a marketplace throughout. The 2012 homepage already showed 30 real prices on the page and led with the name; by 2018 the headline was “Make an offer to any seller or buy it now”; by 2020 it was “The Largest Inventory of Liquor Licenses”; and from 2021 to the latest capture in 2026 it has read “Liquor Licenses for Sale” with no prices on the homepage itself. Marketplace vocabulary outweighs service vocabulary in every year we could probe. Two years, 2016 and 2017, could not be probed at all because every capture in them is a redirect record with no page body.

The “$0k” title defect is recent

We dated it rather than assumed it. Across 104 archived listing pages sampled two per month over 55 months, 101 showed a real price in the page title — “Orange CA, Type 47 Liquor License: $45k” and so on — including both snapshots sampled in June 2026, the most recent month available. Only three showed “$0k”, in April and May 2025. Yet the live census on 12 September 2026 found “$0k” on 1,227 of 1,227 titles. So this is a new regression, most likely introduced between mid-June and mid-September 2026, with a brief earlier appearance in spring 2025. sample, 2 pages per month

A related history note: none of those 104 archived listing pages carried any structured-data markup at all, while the live pages do carry product-and-offer markup today (with the broken context key described in Section 2). Either it was added after the last archived capture, or it is injected by JavaScript that the archive does not run. We cannot tell which from the archive alone.

Section 7

What this report does not settle

The list below is part of the deliverable. Anyone quoting the findings above should know where the evidence stops.

Whether any individual listing is real
Undetermined for 100% of the census, and not determinable from outside while no listing publishes a licence number, licensee name or premises address. Settling it needs one of: the competitor publishing a register key; permission to query the state’s per-county licence-count form, which its own robots file asks crawlers not to fetch; a bulk licence extract; or a public-records request for transfer records.
Whether the missing licence number is policy or a missing database field
Both explanations fit the measurement equally well. Not resolvable from public pages.
Whether the junk records are the whole of the junk
We found 63 distinct placeholder or malformed records using stated thresholds — below $1,000 or above $2,000,000 for a US retail licence. Those thresholds are a stated judgement, not a measurement. A different threshold gives a different count.
Whether their listing index is capped, curated or windowed
Their California index row list stops at 150 rows and New Jersey at 52, with no pagination links observed. Whether that is a fixed limit, a selection or a recency window is not determined.
What the “Views” counter actually is
Established: it is not a faithful record of traffic. Not established: what it is instead. A rolling-window or decorative counter on genuine listings is consistent with boundedness, though it would not explain the near-flat shape. One listing read 11 views in one run and 12 in the next, which is consistent with the counter incrementing on our own fetch — a single observation, not a finding.
Anything about causation
Every visibility figure here is observational. Page shape associates with search visibility in this data. Nothing in it shows that any page type, page count or launch date causes a ranking, and the traffic figures are modelled estimates from a third-party provider rather than measured sessions.
Anything requiring JavaScript to appear
The page-anatomy measurements read raw HTML only, with no JavaScript executed — deliberately, because that is what a text-only crawler sees. A surface added purely by client-side script after load would be counted as absent. The listing pages carried their main content in raw HTML on all 16 sampled pages, so this risk is low there.
Two figures in the source files that we corrected
The census summary line states 67 junk records; adding 25 impossible prices to 42 malformed records double-counts four records that are both, so the distinct total is 63. The same file’s summary says 194 counties; three of those buckets are an empty county field, so the number of named counties is 191. We re-derived both from the raw records.

Method

What was a census

All 1,227 listing pages, fetched and parsed individually: prices, counties, licence types, descriptions, images, counters, seller strings, dates, offer mechanisms. Both live sitemaps in full (3,868 competitor URLs). The competitor’s robots file. Their seller-intake, registration and login forms. A census is a complete count of the population, so these figures carry no sampling error.

What was sampled

Page-anatomy figures — word counts, boilerplate split, text-overlap, images per page — come from 16 listing pages and similar per-type samples, selected deterministically (sorted URLs, every Nth), never at random; the sample size is stated wherever such a figure appears. The archived-title check sampled two pages per month over 55 months. Archive age figures cover the 39% of live listings the archive has captured.

What is modelled

Every traffic and visibility figure is a modelled estimate from a third-party SEO data provider, derived from ranking positions and estimated search volumes. They are not measured visits, and nobody outside the competitor’s business can see those. Search volumes from such providers are estimates, not Google Ads figures. Treat them as relative scale, never as a count of visits.

What is inferred

One figure is explicitly an inference and labelled as such: the age of the 61% of live listings the archive never captured, interpolated from listing-ID order. Its direction is robust; its exact percentages are not.

Language

This is observational data about public web pages. Throughout, features associate with outcomes; nothing here demonstrates cause. Every statement about the competitor is a measurement with its method attached, not a characterisation of their business or their intent.
