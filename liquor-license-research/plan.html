Liquor licence search study · the plan

What to do next

The recommendation, the order to deploy real inventory in, and the technical work order — each step argued from the measurements rather than asserted. Every figure here comes from a data file produced in this study. Where the evidence runs out, the page says so.

01

The recommendation, in short

The direction

Put your real inventory — actual licences, with actual asking prices, rendered as rows on the page itself — onto the state pages you already own, and start with New Jersey. Do not start by building a page per licence. Fix the site's plumbing alongside it, in the corrected sequence in section 04 — because the two largest technical items in that sequence would have broken things if shipped as they were first written.

Three measurements make this a recommendation rather than a preference. First, the rival's own results say hub pages carry the traffic and per-licence pages do not: 8 state hub pages carry 57.03% of its modelled traffic and all eight rank for something, while 1,223 of its 1,227 individual listing pages rank for nothing at all. Second, your site has spent almost its entire page budget in the opposite place: 971 city and county pages are about 96% of the site and return about 5% of its modelled traffic. Third, when your own pages were fetched live, they showed 0 priced licences — the shelf is empty, so a visitor who arrives has nothing to respond to.

New Jersey goes first because it is the one large cell in this market where the top of Google is not dominated by a government website, and because the shelf you would have to match there is small: the rival holds 131 New Jersey licences and shows only 52 of them on its New Jersey page.

02

Why inventory on state pages, and not more pages

The instinct in this market is to build one page per licence and let volume do the work. That approach has already been run at full scale by the rival, and the result is measurable. Here is what each strand of evidence says, independently of the others.

  • 1. Their page types are not equal

    Across a census of its ranking pages: 8 state hubs give 57.03% of modelled traffic; 30 state-by-licence-type pages give 16.57%; 1,227 per-licence pages give 1.77%; 1,692 county-by-type pages give 0.12%.

    Page count and payoff run in opposite directions. Building the big per-item surface is the thing that measurably did not work for them.

  • 2. The keywords you are missing sit on hubs

    Of the 549 keywords where the rival appears in Google and you do not, its state hub template accounts for 36.2% of the search volume and 65.3% of the modelled traffic. Its per-licence pages account for 0.6% of volume and 2.4% of traffic, from 16 keywords.

    Two-thirds of what you are losing, you are losing to one page shape.

  • 3. Demand is at state level, not county level

    Inside the eight states either party operates in, measured demand runs roughly 15.2 to 1 in favour of state-level phrasing over county-level. Only 46 of the rival's 191 county cells carry any measured demand at all; of the 60 counties where it holds exactly one licence, only 5 carry any.

    Its thin county coverage therefore reads as a correct assessment of the market, not an opening. Putting stock into single-listing counties would be putting it where nobody is searching.

  • 4. Your own site already proves it

    971 city and county pages hold 113 ranking keywords and 26.2 of your 491.4 modelled monthly visits — 5.3%. A handful of static and state pages carry the rest, including one orphaned page carrying 60 on its own.

    Same conclusion, reached from the opposite direction, on your data rather than theirs.

  • 5. Your state pages are the thinnest thing you own

    Your 3 measured state hubs carry a median of 32 unique words of body text. The rival's state hub template carries a median of 1,099 total words and 528 unique — about 16.5 times more.

    Your city and county pages are not the problem: their medians are 868.5 and 842 unique words. They need inventory rows, not more prose.

  • 6. Their shelf is real, but shallow and undated

    Their New Jersey page renders 52 genuine prices inline, verified one-to-one against 52 distinct listing links. But across all 1,227 listings there are only 132 distinct description texts, 0 carry any date, 0 publish a licence number, and 6 images serve the whole book.

    The shelf is matchable. The substance behind it is not defended.

Where two of our own files disagree

An earlier file in this study, produced six minutes before the scoring matrix, recommended California first — on the grounds that your California hub is your single biggest structural gap against the rival's highest-yield template. The later scoring matrix puts New Jersey first, because it prices two things the earlier file did not: how much of the top of Google is already held by a government site, and how many licences you would have to put on the page to match what a visitor actually sees. We recommend the later file's order, and the arithmetic is in the next section. The disagreement is real and is recorded here rather than smoothed over.

03

The deployment order

The study scored 317 candidate places to put inventory — combinations of state, licence type and county. 159 have any measured search demand; 97 sit inside the eight states where either party is measurably present, and 62 are held back because neither party is. The score multiplies four measured things: the demand in the cell, how thinly the rival stocks it, how much room is left at the top of Google once government sites have taken their slots, and whether you already rank there at all. Demand is square-root dampened, so a cell ten times larger scores about three times higher rather than ten — one page captures a smaller share of a big search term than of a small one.

The column to read alongside demand is the government ceiling: the share of that cell's measured demand where a government website already holds a top-three position. That figure explains the running order more than demand does. A state regulator's own licence-types page is not something a broker outranks; where the ceiling is high, the commercial slots simply start further down the page.

Why the biggest demand pool is not first

California is the largest pool by a distance: 290 in-scope keywords, 16,560 raw monthly searches, 9,345 after weighting — about 1.7 times New Jersey. It still comes fifth, for two measured reasons that pull the same way.

  • The top of the page is already taken. A government site holds a top-three slot on 94.4% of California's measured demand, and the number-one slot on 93.2%. In New Jersey those figures are 47.4% and 46.5%. In the scoring model that becomes a headroom multiplier of 0.20 for California against 0.60 for New Jersey — a three-fold difference that more than cancels California's demand advantage once demand is square-root dampened.
  • The shelf costs three times as much to match. The rival holds 734 California licences and shows 150 of them on its California page. Matching that visible shelf means 151 priced California rows. New Jersey needs 53.

California is not being dropped — it is second wave, and it carries an advantage nowhere else has: you already rank #1 for a California head term, from your homepage. Inventory added to a California hub inherits an existing position instead of starting from nothing. The judgement in the order is only that New Jersey converts effort into visible position more cheaply, and it is a judgement about an association between these factors and rankings, not a forecast.

There is a second, larger “biggest pool” worth naming, because it is deliberately excluded. Scored purely on open field, the highest-scoring cells in the entire matrix are states where the rival holds nothing at all: Indiana (score 29.07, 10,650 raw monthly searches), New York (22.96, 7,080), Tennessee (20.05, 5,030) and Texas (17.57, 6,930). All are held out of the ranked list. Liquor licensing is state-by-state, and an empty cell in a state where neither party is measurably present is not an opening — it is a different business. If your stock actually reaches those states, those 62 held-out cells become live and the list should be re-scored. That is a question for you, not for the data: nothing measured here says which states your licences sit in.

The one thing this order depends on and cannot verify

Every “rows you need” figure assumes you can put that many real, currently available licences on a page at a real asking price. That you hold more stock than the rival, obtained earlier, some of it off-market, is taken as given from you and is verified nowhere in this study. Check each target against the actual book before committing to it. If New Jersey cannot field 53 priced rows, the first cell changes, and the order should be re-read off the table rather than off this paragraph.

04

The technical work order

Separate from inventory, the site has a structural problem that limits what any new content can do. Of your 1,007 pages, 908 tell Google “the real version of me lives at a different address” — and not one of those 908 real addresses appears in any sitemap. The sitemap is advertising the copy the site itself disowns. Google has already resolved it in the site's favour: 99 of your 244 ranking keywords sit on the real addresses and only 12 on the advertised ones. So the fix is to point the sitemap at the addresses Google already prefers and leave each page's own declaration alone. The alternative — flipping 908 declarations to match the sitemap — would ask Google to move 99 working keywords onto addresses it has already declined. It was considered and rejected.

Read this before the task list

An earlier version of this work order was attacked by 24 independent reviewers — three each against eight of the ten tasks, each instructed to break the task from a different angle and to test every claim against the live site. Every one of the eight tasks was refuted. None survived. Twenty-three of the twenty-four individual reviews returned a refutation. Most were verification failures: the goal was right, but the test the task proposed for “did this work” could not have caught a bad outcome. Two would have caused real damage if shipped as written. What follows is the corrected version, and both are named in full.

Damage case 1 — a redirect that would have closed an infinite loop on 41 pages

The original final task said: once the sitemap change is confirmed, redirect all 908 old addresses to their real ones. An 11-page sample had found the real addresses all returned a normal page, so the task was written as though all 908 did. A reviewer insisted on probing all 908. Of them, 859 are fine — but 49 already redirect somewhere, and 41 of those redirect back to the very address the new redirect would have pointed away from. Shipping it would have closed a loop on 41 pages: a browser or crawler bouncing between two addresses until it gave up. Six of the 41 currently rank for 16 keywords between them, and five are linked from 1,046 pages of your own site. A further 8 redirect to a different page altogether, which would have produced a three-hop chain onto content you did not intend to advertise. Worse, the task's own success test — “each old address returns exactly one redirect to its target” — would have reported all 41 loops as a pass. Corrected: scope drops from 908 to at most 859, the 49 become a separate exception register to be fixed first, and the test now has to detect a loop as a loop.

Damage case 2 — a robots.txt change that would have exempted AI crawlers from your own access rules

The original task said: add lines to robots.txt naming six AI crawlers and allowing them, because an AI answer box appears on 90.9% of the queries measured and the rival is cited far more often than you are. The problem is how the robots standard works. Your file today has a single rule block covering all crawlers: allow everything, except /register, /login and /sg-admin/. Under the standard, a crawler that finds a block naming itself obeys only that block and inherits nothing from the general one. Writing “GPTBot: allow everything” as its own block would therefore have handed those six crawlers access to your login, registration and admin paths — the three places the site deliberately keeps crawlers out of. Two further corrections came from the same review: the six crawlers are already allowed today under the general rule, so the change makes an existing yes explicit rather than granting anything new; and the original success test (byte-compare the general block) could not have detected the failure, because the general block stays unchanged while the new blocks override it. Corrected: either add the six names to the existing block, or repeat all four rules inside every new block — and test by running a real robots parser over the result, seven crawler identities against five paths, 35 assertions.

The sequence below is the corrected order. Two things moved. The duplicate-title task went from fourth to last, because until the old addresses are redirected each of those clusters is four live addresses rather than two, and a fix issued earlier would fight the redirect task for the same URLs. And the orphaned-page task turned out to be a navigation fix rather than a sitemap fix, because the address it named is itself a redirect whose destination is already in the sitemap.

What stays private until a buyer qualifies

The pattern the rival uses, and the one worth keeping: the page is fully public, the deal is gated. Nothing on its side is hidden from Google — its gate is a form, not an index barrier. The line to draw is not between “page” and “no page”. It is between facts a buyer needs to evaluate a licence and facts that identify a seller.

  • The register licence number — gated, and the sharpest open question in the model. In California the register is itself public and keyed by this number, so the number is not secret information. But publishing it against a “for sale” claim additionally discloses that an identifiable business is selling, which is a different act from the register's own publication. The recommendation is to release it to an identified, qualified buyer at diligence under the listing agreement's confidentiality terms — never on a public page, and never for off-market stock. We do not assert that a rule exists here. It plausibly turns on the individual seller mandate and on state advertising rules for licence transfers. This one goes to your attorney. Worth noting: the rival's own seller form requires this number, and it publishes it on 0 of 1,227 listings.
  • Never public: the licensee's legal name, the premises street address, the seller's contact details, their reason for selling, your commission and mandate terms, time on market, and any view or bid counter. The first three identify the business outright; combined with “for sale” that is the disclosure a seller is least likely to have authorised. Publish the municipality instead for public stock, and only the county for private stock. Time on market is a negotiating signal against your own principal. The counters are dealt with below.
  • Aggregate only: what comparable licences sold for, how many off-market licences sit in a cell, their price band, and the “new this month” count. Each is publishable as a number over three or more licences and suppressed below that, because a single transaction plus a month plus a price band can identify the deal. A band must span at least two price buckets or ±15% around the cell median, whichever is wider — at one licence, a band is the price. Publish one band per cell per refresh and keep no running history, or a determined observer can back out individual prices from the changes over time.
  • Generated, never hand-written. Every aggregate is an assertion of fact about your book and has to be defensible if challenged. A stale “7 added since August” is a false statement about the book. At zero, render nothing at all — not “0 available”. The rival's 1,692 mostly-empty county-by-type pages return 0.12% of its traffic.
  • Nothing in the markup that is not on the page. No licence number, name, address or exact off-market price in structured data, hidden elements, meta tags or data attributes while the visible page shows only a teaser. And no special treatment for search engines: the crawler sees exactly what an anonymous visitor sees.

Two fields the rival publishes should not be copied. Its view counter reads as manufactured: bounded between 0 and 36 with every value in that range present, no correlation with listing age, one listing showing a bid against zero views — and the counter on one page moved from 2 to 5 between the census and a single later fetch. A number any visitor can move is not evidence of anything. Its bid counter reads “Bids 0” on 1,199 of 1,227 pages, advertising its own illiquidity on every listing.

The write-once rule, and why it is the whole content model

The rival's 1,227 listing pages contain 8,353 words of distinct writing, served as 78,848 — a 9.4-fold duplication multiple. One California Type 20 description serves all 185 of its California Type 20 listings, and 96% of the 1,198 listings that carry a description share that text with a sibling (29 carry none at all). Its genuinely substantive material — the New Jersey transfer walkthrough, the real fee schedules — is repeated verbatim on all 131 New Jersey listings, so 131 pages compete with each other for the same searches instead of one page winning them.

The fix is structural: 12 blocks of substantive content each live on exactly one address — the licence-class definition and transfer scope on a state-by-type page; the transfer walkthrough, fee schedule, timeline, qualification rules, document checklist, escrow mechanics, local permits, verification how-to and risk factors on one guide page per state — and listings carry a short excerpt plus a link, never the full block. This is also what puts one page in front of the two largest demand pools in this market: process and how-to searches at 46,410 a month, and cost searches at 14,310, both larger than the “for sale” pool at 11,290.

The gate that stops the rival's mistake being repeated

A licence gets its own page only if it can fill 150 words that appear nowhere else on your site, measured after stripping the shared blocks. Those 150 words come from five things that genuinely vary per licence: where it sits and what it covers, where its price falls within its own cell, its specific transfer conditions, what a buyer must already have in place for that cell, and its status and expected timeline. If a licence cannot fill all five with true statements, it renders as a row on the hub and gets no page of its own. Overlap between any two listings in the same cell is capped, the shared excerpt is capped at 200 words, and the unique block must be at least 35% of the substantive text. These are build-blocking checks, not style guidance. For calibration: your existing city and county templates already achieve overlap scores roughly 10 times better than the cap, so this is comfortably inside what your site already does.

Word budgets follow from the same logic. A state hub carrying inventory needs at least 1,099 total and 528 unique words — the measured median of the rival template that carries 57% of its traffic — against your current median of 32. A public listing page needs 350 to 500 words: deliberately fewer in total than the rival's median listing page, which measures 1,097.5 words on a 16-page sample and 1,374 across the full 1,227-page census — and about twice as many of them unique to that licence.

One more field deserves naming, because it is the clearest single content opening measured: the moratorium. Where a county has frozen new issuance of a licence type, a transfer is the only route to one — which is the entire reason your stock has value. A state report already in hand covers 58 counties, 40 of them under a moratorium for one California licence type. 0 of the rival's 1,198 descriptions mention it, while it holds 185 listings of exactly that type. The report in hand is from 2023 and must be re-pulled before anything is published from it.

06

Unresolved — what needs you, or a lawyer

This list is part of the deliverable, not an omission from it. Each item is something the measurements cannot settle, and several of them gate work described above.

  • For your attorney

    Advertising a licence transfer is regulated, and this study does not rule on it

    Advertising the transfer of an alcoholic-beverage licence carries state regulatory exposure. California's regulator governs how transfers are advertised and escrowed, and several states restrict who may broker a licence and how an offer may be presented. A gated private tier may carry its own disclosure duties — what a prospective buyer must be told, and when. Publishing a real count and a real price band of off-market stock is an assertion of fact about your book and must be defensible if challenged.

    Whether the register licence number may be published for public-tier stock is the sharpest open question in the content model. We recommend gating it and we assert no rule. None of this is legal advice; no opinion is offered on whether any element is permissible in any state; and nothing here is clearance to publish.

  • For your attorney

    Using the rival's pages as a source, and reproducing regulator text

    Its robots file permits crawling across all 3,868 of its addresses — that is measured. It is not permission. A robots file is a crawling convention, not a copyright licence and not terms of service. Three constraints apply and only the first is settled: robots (open, measured), terms of service (unread and unassessed here), and copyright — its 132 description texts are short, but they are original expression. The recommendation is to take facts from regulators and write the sentences fresh, and never to copy or closely paraphrase its text.

    Separately: US federal government works are generally outside copyright, but state government works are not uniformly so and vary by state. Verify per state before reproducing any regulator text verbatim, and prefer paraphrase plus a citation and a link in every case.
