LLLiquor licence search research /What we found

The main report

What we found

Two websites sell into the same market. One of them gets roughly eleven times the search traffic of the other. This report explains where that traffic actually sits, why the obvious explanations do not hold up, and what the evidence says works here — including the two hard ceilings on anything either site builds.

All figures measured 12 September 2026 unless stated. Read the method note at the foot of the page before quoting any number.

  • 57.0% of the rival's search traffic comes from just 8 pages — one per state. Census · ranked.json
  • 1.8% comes from its 1,227 individual licence-for-sale listing pages. Census · ranked.json

  • 81.2% of the searches we tested put a government page in the top three. Sample, n=197 · serp.json
Before the numbers

Three words you need, and one warning

Everything below rests on three ideas. They are worth ninety seconds.

A page template
A repeated page design that gets filled with different data. "One page per state" is a template; so is "one page per licence for sale". A site with 3,868 pages might have only twelve templates. Grouping pages by template is how you see which kind of page earns its keep.
Modelled traffic (also called estimated traffic)
Not visitors. It is a calculation: for every search a page ranks for, take the estimated monthly search volume, multiply by the share of clicks a page in that position typically gets, and add it all up. It is the best available comparison when you cannot see either site's analytics — but it is a model, and we label it as one every time.
A census versus a sample
A census counts everything. A sample counts some things and lets you infer the rest. We say which one every figure is. The ranked-keyword data is a census of the supplier's index; the 197 live searches are a sample.

The warning, and it applies to every finding on this page.

This is observational search data. We can show that a certain kind of page is sitting in the positions that get the clicks. We cannot show that the page design caused it to get there. Other explanations we could not hold constant include domain age, link authority, internal linking, how much text each page carries, how quickly each site gets indexed, and brand recognition. Several of those we measured separately and report below; none is fully ruled out. Read every finding as "associates with", never "causes".

Finding 1

The rival's traffic sits on eight pages, not on its 3,868

The rival, liquorlicenseauctioneers.com, publishes 3,868 pages in its sitemap. We pulled every search term its pages rank for — 713 of them, a complete census of the supplier's index for that site, not a sample — grouped every ranking page by its template, and added up the modelled traffic per template.

The result is lopsided in a way that changes what this market looks like. Eight pages — one per state, at addresses as plain as /new-jersey and /california — carry 57.0% of the site's modelled search traffic. The 1,227 pages that each advertise one licence for sale, the actual inventory, carry 1.8%. Another 1,692 pages combining a county with a licence type carry 0.1%.

The same table with the underlying counts, because the share alone hides how thoroughly some of these templates fail. "Pages that rank" means pages appearing in the search results for at least one term. "Top-3 placements" counts every instance of a page from that template sitting in the first three organic results.

Two details sharpen it. First, the concentration is tighter than "eight pages": a single page, /new-jersey, carries 1,970 of the site's 5,471 modelled monthly visits — 36.0% of the whole site from one address. Eleven pages account for 80% of it. Second, the 17 search terms the listing pages do rank for are almost all place-and-cost queries rather than inventory queries: eleven of them are variations on "liquor license miami", "miami liquor license cost", "how much is a liquor license in miami dade county". The listing pages are not winning because they are listings. They are winning a handful of Miami-area searches.

The rival's page count is not the asset. Its state hubs are. Of 3,868 published pages, only 112 appear in search results at all — 2.9%. The other 3,756 are, on this measurement, invisible.

Finding 2

Both sites publish exactly eight state hub pages. One set wins 49 top-three spots. The other wins none.

This is the strongest single piece of evidence in the engagement, because it removes the usual objection. You cannot say "the rival wins because it has more pages" when, on the page type that carries most of its traffic, the two sites publish the same number.

The client, liquorlicenseagents.com, publishes 1,007 pages. Eight of them are state-level hubs. The rival publishes 3,868 pages, and eight of them are state-level hubs. Same template count, same day, same measurement method, both read from the same supplier endpoint. Here is what those sixteen pages do.

Zero is an unusual number in this kind of research, so it is worth being precise about it. The client's eight state hubs are not absent from search — three of them rank for thirty terms between them. Not one of those thirty rankings falls inside the top ten results. The best of them, /california/liquor-license/, reaches seventeen search terms worth 4,540 monthly searches and converts them into 9.5 modelled visits, because every one of those seventeen positions sits below tenth place.

One honest difference in the eight-versus-eight comparison

The page counts match; the coverage does not. The rival's eight hubs cover eight distinct states, one page each. The client's eight cover six states, with three of the eight pointed at California (/california, /california/california-liquor-license/ and /california/liquor-license/). So the comparison is fair on page count and on method, but the two sets are not identically shaped. We read that as part of the finding rather than a flaw in it: three competing pages on one state is a structural choice, and the state where the client has three hub pages is the state where it has zero top-ten placements.

The same comparison run a second way, on identical search terms

A different objection is that the two sites simply chase different search terms, so their totals cannot be compared. That objection is testable. From the same data pull, 164 search terms have both sites appearing in the results. On those 164 — identical searches, identical results pages, no difference in keyword selection possible — the rival ranks higher on 138 of them and the client on 26. That is 84.1% to the rival, with a median gap of 22 places.

Nor is it about geographic spread. The rival covers eight states and the client is concentrated in California, so the obvious read is that the rival simply plays a bigger board. It does not hold: California is the rival's strongest state, carrying 56 of its 132 state-level top-3 placements — 42.4%. On the highest-volume California terms the pattern is stark. For "liquor license california" (1,000 monthly searches) the rival's state hub sits at position 3 while the client's nearest page sits at 43. For "how much is a liquor license in california" (880 searches) the rival's blog article sits at 3 and the client's California hub at 41.

What this comparison establishes, and where it stops.

It establishes that the gap is not explained by page count, keyword selection or geographic coverage — the three easiest explanations — because each was held constant in turn and the gap survived. It does not establish that the hub template is the cause. A page's design, its text, its internal links and the authority of the domain it sits on all travel together, and this measurement cannot separate them.

Finding 3

It is not links. On links, the client is ahead.

The standard explanation for a gap this size is authority: the other site has more links from more websites, so it wins. We measured it, on identical endpoints, on the same day, for both domains. The explanation does not survive contact with the data, and the direction of the numbers is the reason.

Read the table as a whole and the shape is unusual. The client has 1.75 times as many referring websites, a near-identical total link count, a higher backlink strength score, and a distinctly cleaner link profile — 77.8% of its links pass authority against the rival's 39.1%, and its links come overwhelmingly from .com addresses while 46.9% of the rival's come from .ru domains. And it receives roughly a ninth of the search traffic.

A gap that runs in the opposite direction to the link counts cannot be explained by those link counts. Whatever separates these two sites, this measurement did not find it in domain-level link authority.

We did not stop at the pair. Across the seven liquor-licence sites we measured, we tested every possible ordered pairing — 42 of them — and asked how often the less-linked site has the more search traffic. Seven pairings out of 42 (16.7%) run backwards. Among those seven: the rival beats the client on traffic with 1.75× fewer referring websites; gloverlaw.net beats the client on traffic by 6.8× with 1.41× fewer referring websites; the rival beats gloverlaw.net with 1.24× fewer. Rank the seven niche sites by referring websites and by traffic and the two orders agree only weakly — a rank correlation of 0.46. Add three national giants (reddit.com, ca.gov, myfloridalicense.com) and the correlation jumps to 0.82, but that is arithmetic about sites three to six orders of magnitude larger, not information about this market.

What the authority finding proves, and three things it does not.

It proves that "the rival just has more links" is false as stated, on this measurement, for this pair. It also proves that the barrier is small in absolute terms: both sides sit in double- and low-triple-digit referring domains. Neither holds a link moat.

It does not prove that links do not matter. It is a single-day snapshot, and ranking well earns links, so the rival's links are as plausibly a consequence of ranking as a cause of it.

It does not tell us where the links point. Domain-level counts cannot distinguish "the marketplace design attracts links to listing pages" from "an older domain has a well-linked homepage". Settling that needs a page-level link pull, which was not bought.

It does not rule out age. The rival's domain is about 3.1 years older. That is a real head start, though not the decade-scale asymmetry that would make the gap structural on age alone — and within the window the supplier can see (from February 2019), only 1.1% of the rival's current referring domains were already in place at the start, so most of its measurable link profile was built recently rather than compounded over a decade.

Finding 4

It was not always this way — and the rival is now shrinking

We pulled 71 consecutive months of visibility for both domains, October 2020 to August 2026, from the same endpoint with the same settings. Two things in that series matter commercially.

The first: in October 2020 the client was ahead. It appeared in the top ten results for 102 search terms against the rival's 70, and its modelled traffic was 844 a month against the rival's 782. The rival overtook it on modelled traffic in November 2020 and on top-ten placements in March 2021. What exists today is the result of roughly five years of divergence, not a permanent feature of the market.

The second: both curves are currently falling, and the rival's is falling faster in absolute terms. Over the trailing twelve months the rival's total ranked terms went from 1,227 to 749, a slope of about −41 terms a month. Over twenty-four months, 2,544 to 749. The client's fell from 391 to 225 over twelve months. Neither site is compounding right now.

One nuance that keeps the decline honest. The rival's total ranked terms are falling sharply, but its top-ten placements have held roughly flat between about 300 and 350 since early 2022 — it finished August 2026 at 301, against a December 2021 peak of 462. Its losses are concentrated in the long tail of positions nobody clicks, not in the positions that earn traffic. Its modelled traffic in August 2026, 6,357 a month, is higher than it was in most of 2022. So "the rival is shrinking" is true of its keyword footprint and not yet true of its commercial visibility.

A note on two numbers that disagree slightly.

This section says the rival ranks for 749 terms; Finding 1 says 713. They come from different endpoints at the same supplier — a monthly historical series against a live ranked-keyword pull — and neither is corrected against the other. A few percent of disagreement between two views of the same index is normal and we report both rather than averaging them.

Finding 5

The words a broker would choose are the smallest pool of demand in the market

Separately from who ranks, we sized the demand itself: what people actually type. We pulled the keyword universe from twenty seed phrases, screened out the searches that are about alcohol but not about licensing, and sorted what remained into intent families by an explicit, reproducible rule.

The screening step matters and we want it visible. The first pull was dominated by searches that contain the word "alcohol" but have nothing to do with premises licences — drink-driving limits, blood-alcohol levels, court-ordered testing, drinking age. Removing them cut 56.1% of the pulled search volume, leaving 3,964 in-scope search terms worth 195,610 monthly searches. Everything below is drawn from that screened set.

Three things follow, and they cut in different directions.

Broker language is the smallest commercial pool in the market. Searches naming a broker, agent or consultant total 35 terms and 3,210 monthly searches — 1.64% of in-scope demand. Buying-side searches alone are 3.5 times larger by volume (11,290 a month across 229 terms); buying and selling together are 3.66 times larger (11,760 a month across 248 terms). Selling on its own is the smallest family in the market, at 470 a month across 19 terms. If a business describes itself the way its customers do not search, its best-written pages compete for a pool this size.

But that small pool is the most valuable per search, and it is where the client is closest to winning. Broker-language searches carry the highest advertiser cost-per-click in the set — a median of $7.65 against $3.57 for buying-side searches — which is the market's own signal that these searches convert. And on the live results we pulled, the twenty-one service-intent searches are the only intent family of any size where the client is ahead of the rival: the client appears in the top ten on 28.6% of them against the rival's 23.8%. For comparison, on the forty buying-intent searches the rival appears in the top ten on 97.5% and the client on 2.5%; on the thirty cost searches, 100% against 0%. Its single best-performing page is its homepage, at position 1 for "liquor license broker california" and "liquor license brokers california" and position 1 for "liquor license agent".

The money is in the generic middle, and nobody owns it. The largest pool by far is neither buying nor selling nor broking: 1,979 terms and 101,320 monthly searches that are simply a place plus the words "liquor license" — "indiana liquor license", "nyc liquor license", "liquor license texas". Both page types compete for these, and this is exactly the pool the rival's eight state hubs are built to answer.

The keyword-gap data says the same thing from the other side. Of the 549 search terms the rival ranks for and the client does not — a census of that endpoint's index — the largest slice by modelled traffic is programmatic geography-and-type pages at 91.0% of the gap's traffic value. Broker-service language accounts for 8 terms and 610 monthly searches: the smallest family in the gap. The gap is not that the client has failed to write about being a broker. It is that the client is not present on place-plus-licence searches.

Finding 6, the first ceiling

Nine in ten of these searches now show an AI answer, and the rival is cited in most of them

We ran 197 live searches and recorded what Google actually displayed. An AI overview — the generated answer box above the ordinary results — appeared on 179 of 197 searches, 90.9%.

That 90.9% is a floor, not a measurement.

Google loads some AI overviews after the page first renders. Capturing those costs extra per search and was not purchased, so overviews that load late are undercounted. The true prevalence is 90.9% or higher. Anyone quoting this figure should quote it as a floor.

The citation counts carry a second, larger caveat, and it is worth stating plainly because it halves the usable sample. Of the 179 searches showing an overview, only 102 returned a readable list of the sources the overview cited. The other 77 were flagged as late-loading and returned no sources at all. So the citation figures below are computed on 102 searches, not 197 and not 179.

On the readable subset, the rival is cited seven times as often as the client. That is worth stating carefully: an AI citation is a mention, not a click, and we did not measure whether any of it produces business. What it does mean is that on a market where nine in ten searches show a generated answer first, one of these two businesses is the answer's most-quoted commercial source and the other is barely present.

Two features that are absent are worth recording, because their absence is also information. Across all 197 searches, Google served a featured snippet on zero of them and paid ads on zero of them. A local map pack appeared on 24 (12.2%) and a "people also ask" block on 189 (95.9%).

Finding 7, the second ceiling

Government pages take the top of these results, and no commercial page can change that

Anyone planning what to build in this market needs to know how much of the first page is already spoken for. We classified every one of the 1,970 top-ten results across our 197 searches by the kind of business that owns the domain. Government regulators are the largest holder of the positions that matter.

Across all 197 searches, government domains hold 535 of 1,970 top-ten slots (27.2%) — and 261 of 591 top-three slots (44.2%). A government page appears in the top three on 160 of 197 searches, 81.2%. Marketplace-type businesses hold 16.2% of top-ten slots and 28.8% of top-three. Service businesses hold the most slots overall, 31.6%, but only 16.8% of the top three, at a mean position of 6.3.

On an average search here, 1.3 of the three most-clicked results are already a regulator's own page. The commercial contest is for what is left.

That ceiling is not uniform, and where it is lower is where commercial pages do best. We grouped the 131 place-specific searches that name somewhere inside one of the eight states in play, and counted.

The pattern across the eight is legible. New Jersey and Florida have the weakest government ceilings — a regulator holds the top three on 17.5% and 25.0% of slots against Arizona's 63.9% — and they are where the rival is most completely dominant. Of New Jersey's 57 measured top-three slots the rival takes 18 and the regulator 10; of Florida's 48, the rival takes 11 and the regulator 12. New Jersey is also the rival's single most valuable page. The relationship runs the way commercial logic suggests: where the regulator publishes less, a commercial page takes the space.

California is the opposite case and it is the client's own market. A government page holds the number-one result on 54 of 58 California searches we measured. The rival still takes 29 top-three slots there; the client takes 2. So the ceiling is real but it is not the client's binding constraint — the rival is operating under the same ceiling and clearing far more of what sits beneath it.

A final confirmation from a different measurement. We took the 50 highest-volume searches the rival ranks for and the client does not, and read a live results page for each. A government domain held a top-three slot on 36 of the 50. A marketplace-type page held top-three on 2. On the highest-value searches in the gap, the first page is mostly regulators.

Finding 8

What this evidence does not settle

This list is part of the deliverable. Anyone who reads the findings above and not this section will over-read them.

  1. Whether the hub template causes the rankings. All of this is observational. We held page count, keyword selection and geographic coverage constant and the gap survived each. We did not and could not hold constant per-page text depth, internal linking, indexation speed, brand recognition, or how completely the supplier's index covers each domain. The hub template is strongly associated with the positions that earn traffic. It is not proven to cause them.
  2. Whether links matter here. We can say "the rival just has more links" is false for this pair on this measurement. We cannot say links are irrelevant. Ranking well earns links, so the direction of causation between the rival's links and its rankings is untested, and a page-level link pull — which would show whether the rival's links point at its hubs or its homepage — was not bought.
  3. What a duplicate-address problem is costing the client. A large finding sits half-finished. 46.7% of the client's ranked search terms — 114 of 244 — rank on a web address that is absent from its own sitemap. The site serves the same page at two live addresses, a nested one it declares (/cities/agoura-hills/agoura-hills-liquor-licenses…) and a flat one it does not (/cities/agoura-hills-liquor-licenses…), and the supplier recorded the flat one as the ranking address. The rival shows nothing equivalent: zero such rows. What we did not test is whether both addresses return a page, whether either declares the other as the preferred version, and which one Google treats as canonical. Until that is tested we cannot say whether this is costing the client anything. It is the cheapest unresolved question on the list.
  4. The true prevalence of AI answers, and of AI citations. 90.9% is a floor. Citation shares rest on 102 searches, not 197, because 77 overviews loaded too late to read. Buying the asynchronous capture would settle both.
  5. Whether a top-ten position we recorded is still a top-ten position. Two clocks run through this research. The ranked-keyword, gap and history data come from a weekly-refreshed index; the 197 SERP pulls and the 50-keyword gap check are live reads. Where we could compare the two on the same terms, 18 of 23 agreed within two places — but on 27 of the 50 top gap keywords the rival was absent from the live top ten despite the index recording a ranking. Treat index positions as approximately right, not exactly right.
  6. Anything about revenue, leads or conversion. Every traffic figure on this page is modelled, not measured. No analytics, no Search Console, no call or form data from either site entered this research. Modelled traffic is a defensible way to compare two sites you cannot see inside; it is not a forecast of anyone's sessions, and it says nothing about what converts.
  7. Whether the market is growing or shrinking. Both curves are falling over the trailing twelve and twenty-four months. We did not measure total category demand over time, so we cannot say whether that is two sites losing ground or a market contracting around them.
  8. How much of this generalises past our sample. The 197 searches were chosen by an explicit priority rule — seeded head terms first, then terms the rival ranks 1–3 for, then terms the client ranks 1–20 for — and not at random. That rule is reproducible and published, but it tilts the sample towards terms at least one of these two sites already touches. Small state cells (New Mexico at three searches, Pennsylvania at five) cannot support a state-level conclusion on their own.
  9. The third domain. usaliquorlicense.com was measured and is effectively invisible: 20 published pages, 14 ranked terms, none in the top twenty, 38 referring websites, and roughly 2 modelled visits a month. It is recorded here for completeness, not analysed.
In one paragraph

What the evidence adds up to

The rival does not outrank the client because it publishes more pages, because it chases different search terms, because it covers more states, or because it has more links — each of those was tested and each failed. What separates the two sites, on this evidence, is that eight plain state-level pages on the rival's domain occupy 49 top-three positions and carry 57.0% of its search traffic, while the client's eight equivalent pages occupy none. The market's largest pool of demand is neither buying nor broking but 101,320 monthly searches that are simply a place plus the words "liquor license" — the exact question a state hub answers. Two ceilings bound anything built against it: a regulator's own page sits in the top three on roughly four in five of these searches, and nine in ten now open with a generated AI answer in which the rival is the most-cited commercial source and the client is cited on one search in ten. None of this is proof of cause, and the list above says exactly where it stops.

How this was measured

All data was pulled on 12 September 2026 from DataForSEO, for the United States (location code 2840) in English. Every domain was pulled from the same endpoints with the same parameters on the same day, so no site was measured more deeply than another. Where a figure is our own calculation rather than a published field, the caption says so and names the file.

What is a census

The ranked-keyword data for all three domains (713, 244 and 14 terms), the keyword-gap sets (549, 164 and 80 terms), the 71-month history for both domains, and the link counts. Each retrieved every row the supplier's index holds — complete for that index, which is not the same as complete for the web.

What is a sample

The 197 live searches (chosen by a published priority rule, not at random), the 50-keyword live check on the top gap terms, and the keyword universe (3,964 in-scope terms from twenty seed phrases — a different seed set returns a different universe).

Which numbers are estimates

Every search volume, cost-per-click and difficulty score is a DataForSEO estimate built on Google Ads data — not a Google Ads figure. Every "modelled visits" figure is a calculation from position and volume, not measured sessions. No analytics or Search Console data from any site was used.

Association, not cause

This is observational search data throughout. Findings say a page type is associated with a position. Nothing here shows that a page type caused a position, and the competing explanations we could not hold constant are listed in Finding 8.
