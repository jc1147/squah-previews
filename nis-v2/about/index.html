The story, in writing

Why this desk exists.

I'm James Compton. I spent years inside local marketing: building sites, running campaigns, reading the monthly reports agencies send their clients. And I kept seeing the same thing: the report and the invoice come from the same desk, and the client has no way to check the report. When the people grading the work are the people selling it, and the grade cannot be recomputed by anyone else, the grade is an ad. Small businesses, a chiropractor, an optometrist, a dispensary owner, have no practical way to check whether the visibility they pay for every month actually exists on the map their customers use.

The strange part is that checking is possible. A business's map ranking is not a secret; it is sitting in public, at every street corner, for anyone who scans it. A website's speed is measurable by anyone with the URL. Whether a "custom" site is one of six hundred copies of the same template is visible in the page source. None of this requires inside access. It requires instruments, patience, and no financial reason to look away.

So that became the desk. I measure an agency's entire client book, every client I can find, not a sample, not the three success stories on the agency's homepage, with the same instruments applied the same way to every business. Then I publish the whole thing: the method, the counts, and every figure, in a public dossier. If an agency's results are good, the file says so. So far, the files have said something else. And yes, I compete with the agencies in these files. That is exactly why I could afford to measure every one of their 906 clients instead of writing an opinion. The difference between this desk and a competitor's hit piece is that you can check any number in it.

If the numbers were wrong, the fastest way to end this desk would be to check one. That is an invitation.

Where I stand

I own marketing agencies, and I built SGEN, a software stack that runs marketing for businesses like the ones in these files. I am a competitor of the agencies measured here, not of the businesses inside their books, and I want those businesses to see what they are actually getting. Read the files knowing exactly that. It changes nothing about the numbers: I hold no stake in any agency named here, no agency has ever paid this desk, and no agency can pay to be excluded, softened, or included. A file that can be bought is worthless, so the files are not for sale, in either direction. What is for sale, openly, is what comes after the file: if you read your listing and want it fixed, my consultants take the booking. The receipts are free either way.

If you just found your provider in a file

Take a breath. A dossier naming your agency is not a claim that your business is failing; it is a measurement of what your agency delivered across its whole book, with your listing as one data point in it. You are the injured party in these files, not the accused. Your own numbers, your map, your rankings, your speed test, are in the file right now: find your listing and read them at no cost. And understand what they mean: until the person running your map changes what they are doing, the map is not going to change. When you are ready to talk about that, book a consultation. The file stays the same either way; nothing you do or don't buy can touch a published number.

Why publishing this is fair

Results are public.
People are not.

The line this desk holds: an agency's delivered results are legitimate public interest, because they were always sitting in public view. The private information of the businesses involved is not, and never appears.

What the files publish
  • Map rankings, recorded at public map points anyone can stand on and scan.
  • Speed readings, from the same public testing tools Google offers everyone.
  • Template forensics, drawn from page source any browser can view.
  • Aggregate counts, how a whole client book performs, computed from the rows above.
  • The method itself, so every figure can be recomputed by a stranger.
What never appears
  • Owner phone numbers, email addresses, or any contact data gathered during research.
  • Anything obtained from a private account, a leak, or inside access; there is none.
  • Revenue, contracts, billing, or any private business terms.
  • Claims about intent or character. The files measure output, not motive.
  • Paid placements, in either direction. Nothing in a file is for sale.

That asymmetry is deliberate. Agencies market themselves on their clients' results, results which exist in public, on public maps and public websites. Reading those results back to the public, carefully and completely, is fair comment on work that was always on display. The businesses inside each book get the opposite treatment: their data is shown to them, privately and freely, and their contact information is never published, sold, or shared.

01

Roster discovery

Every client an agency serves: assembled from showcase pages, template fingerprints, and the code agencies leave in page source. The whole book, not the highlight reel.

02

Geo-grid scans

Each business is scanned across a grid of real map points around its own location, and its rank is recorded at every pin. 131,285 points recorded so far.

03

Website forensics

The same tests on every site: PageSpeed readings, template reuse, on-page basics. Instrument readings, not opinions; the tools are public and free.

04

Published

Every figure lands in a public dossier and can be recomputed from the steps above. Errors, when found, are corrected on the record with a note saying what changed.

Corrections policy

If any figure in any file is wrong, the desk wants to know more than anyone. Dispute a number through the contact page with the listing it concerns; it is re-measured with the same instruments, and if the file was wrong, the file changes: publicly, with a dated correction note, not a silent edit. A desk that buries its mistakes is running the same con it reports on.
