Med spas reach page one the way other local practices do: a Google Business Profile that wins the map results for treatment searches, a website with one useful page per treatment, and a steady flow of genuine reviews. What sets a med spa apart is the rulebook around its marketing: who may perform a treatment, what "FDA approved" really means, how before-and-after photos may be used, and what Google and Meta allow in health ads.

This guide takes both halves in the order that moves rankings. The ideas here keep paying back: a treatment page written well this year still earns visits next year, while a boosted post stops when the budget does. Where a rule applies, we say what it says, who it binds and when it took effect, and leave how it applies to your practice to your own counsel.

What page one looks like for med spa searches

Direct answer

For many med spa searches, page one has two places a practice can win: the map results, drawn from Google Business Profiles, and the organic results, drawn from websites. Google's AI answer is a third place, but it shows only for some searches. In our Las Vegas check, "med spa" showed map and organic results and no AI answer [1].

A single capture is a snapshot, not a rule, but it shows how differently Google treats two kinds of med spa search. We searched 12 commercial phrases from Las Vegas on September 27, 2026, on desktop, and recorded what page one held [1].

  • "med spa": three med spas in the map results; below them, organic results that mixed med spa websites with Yelp, Facebook and Instagram pages; no AI Overview [1].
  • "botox": an AI Overview citing sources such as MedlinePlus, WebMD and Drugs.com, three local businesses in the map results, and organic results held by botox.com, botoxcosmetic.com and health publishers such as Mayo Clinic and Cleveland Clinic [1].

The AI Overview for "botox" named none of the three businesses in its map results, and across all 12 searches Google showed an AI Overview for only two [1]. The search that read as local left room for practice websites in the organic results; the one that read as a treatment name gave them to the brand and to national health publishers, and none of the three map businesses held an organic slot. A med spa needs both: a profile that can win the map, and pages that can win the organic results for longer, local phrases.

Key takeaway

Measure every phrase in each place separately; the map, the organic results and an AI answer are won with different signals.

Stat

In the American Society of Plastic Surgeons' (ASPS) 2025 figures, neuromodulator injections, commonly referred to as Botox, made up roughly half of all minimally invasive procedures, and hyaluronic acid fillers another 30% [2].

Stat

The American Med Spa Association (AmSpa) reports average med spa revenue of $1,398,833 in 2024, up from $1,307,587 in 2023 [3]. It is a trade-association figure; we could not see the method behind it.

Measure the med spas already on page one

Direct answer

Before you spend on marketing, measure the businesses that already hold page one for your treatments. For the map results, record their categories, reviews, ratings and review pace. For the organic results, record what their pages cover and how many sites link to them. For AI answers, record which pages Google cites. Your plan is the gap.

Google's own advice on hiring an SEO tells business owners to ask what results to expect, in what timeframe, and how success will be measured [4]. The same page is blunt about promises:

We agree with it. Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking [5], and that third-party SEO tools don't have access to its internal ranking data [4]. What you can measure is the gap between your practice and the businesses already on page one, place by place:

A simple way to think about it: for each phrase, find the weakest business or page holding a slot in each place, measure how far you are behind it, and work the smallest gaps first. The phrase list: treatments x modifiers x places

Direct answer

Build your phrase list by multiplying three columns: the treatments you offer, the words people add to them (cost, near me, before and after, for men), and the places you serve. Then check the list against real data: Google's autocomplete, Keyword Planner, your Business Profile's search terms and Search Console. Keep only phrases a real client would type.

TreatmentsModifiersPlaces
Neuromodulator injections, dermal fillers, laser hair removal, chemical peels, microneedling, IV therapy, medical weight lossnear me, cost, price, before and after, for men, reviews, open nowyour city, its neighborhoods, the towns you draw clients from

Start from what you actually offer. Google suggests service options tailored to your business type, and a service listed on your profile may be highlighted when people search for it [9]. For places, Keyword Planner can suggest nearby locations and break results down by location [10]. Google applies the searcher's location even when the query names no place, so a bare treatment name typed in your city can return local results [11].

Then check the list against four sources of real searches:

  1. Autocomplete. Google says its predictions reflect real searches and vary with the location a query comes from [12].
  2. Keyword Planner. Its volumes are averages over close variants, rounded, and tied to the location you choose [13]. Close variants share one number, so never add them together [14].
  3. Your Business Profile. The Performance report lists the search terms people used to find your profile, updated at the start of each month [15].
  4. Search Console. Its queries dimension groups your site's data by the searches people typed [16].

Don't drop a phrase because it shows little or no volume. In Ahrefs' US database, almost 93% of keywords get fewer than 10 searches a month [17]. When we sent 606 phrases to Google Ads for this blog's research, 158 came back with no monthly figure at all [14].

Drug names deserve a note. Google's ads policy lists botox, dysport, xeomin, semaglutide and tirzepatide among prescription drug terms [18], and Google's policy says US campaigns must be certified to keyword-target those terms, though certification isn't needed to use them in ads and landing pages [19]. That rule decides which keywords a campaign can target, which is one more reason to win those phrases in the map and organic results. Treatment pages that answer real questions

Direct answer

Give every treatment its own page: what it is, who performs it and under whose supervision, what the visit involves, recovery, risks, and how price is set. In Whitespark's 2026 survey of local search experts, a dedicated page for each service was the top factor for local organic rankings and the second for AI search visibility [6].

Health pages face a higher bar. Google says: "our systems give even more weight to content that aligns with strong E-E-A-T for topics that could significantly impact the health, financial stability, or safety of people" [20]. E-E-A-T stands for experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust, and Google says trust matters most; it also encourages accurate bylines where readers might expect them [20].

For a med spa, trust starts with who does the work. Name the injector and the supervising physician on every treatment page, with their credentials, and describe the supervision as it actually works in your practice. The rules on who may perform what are set state by state, and two examples show how specific they get:

  • California. The Medical Board of California says physicians may inject Botox or direct registered nurses or physician assistants to do so under their supervision, and that unlicensed people, such as medical assistants, may not [21]. It also says a business offering these services must be a physician-owned medical practice or a professional medical corporation with a physician as the majority shareholder [21]. Its FAQ does not address nurse practitioners.
  • Texas. Jenifer's Law (HB 3749), in effect since September 1, 2025, lets a physician delegate elective IV therapy to a physician assistant, an advanced practice registered nurse, or a registered nurse acting under adequate physician supervision [22].

Your state's rules may differ, and they change. Check your page copy against your state's medical and nursing boards and your own counsel, and describe only what your practice actually does.

Say "cleared" when the FDA cleared it. The FDA says cosmetic products and ingredients don't require FDA approval, and that moderate-risk devices receive "510(k) clearance" [23]. A federal rule says a representation that creates an impression of official approval because a device went through premarket notification "is misleading and constitutes misbranding" [24]. The FDA also says compounded drugs are not FDA approved, and flags sellers who claim a compounded drug is the same as an FDA-approved one [25]. And it recommends against using dermal fillers or any injectable filler for body contouring [26].

Claims need evidence behind them, not just happy clients. The FTC's health products guidance says advertisers should not make claims through testimonials or expert endorsements that would be deceptive, or couldn't be substantiated, if the advertiser made them directly [27]. It is written for health products, so ask your counsel how it applies to treatment claims.

Resist the page-per-town shortcut. Google's spam policies treat pages created to rank for similar searches that funnel visitors to one page as doorway abuse, and pages generated at scale to manipulate rankings as scaled content abuse [28]. One strong page per treatment is the safer build than a thin copy for every nearby town.

Checklist: a treatment page

The Business Profile for a med spa

Direct answer

Your Business Profile decides whether you appear in the map results. Choose the most specific primary category, such as Medical spa, add only the few extra categories that describe what you are, list every treatment as a service, keep hours real and use your real-world name. Google says the categories you choose affect your local ranking [29].

Categories. Google publishes no public list of its categories, so marketers rely on compiled lists. PlePer's US list includes Medical spa, Skin care clinic, Facial spa, Laser hair removal service, IV therapy service, Weight loss service and Tattoo removal service [30]. A profile has one primary category and up to nine more, and the first field is the primary [31]. Google's guidelines say to choose the fewest categories that describe your core business, to pick ones that complete the statement "This business IS a", and never to use categories as keywords [32]. Changing a category can trigger re-verification [29]. In Whitespark's 2026 survey, the primary category was the highest-scoring local pack factor of all [6].

Name. Use the name on your sign. Google's guidelines say to represent your business as it is recognized in the real world, and that adding unnecessary information to the name can get the profile suspended [32]. Whitespark's experts list keyword stuffing in the business name among the leading suspension risks [6].

Services. List every treatment as a service. Google says businesses can add custom services with details such as price and description, and that a listed service may be highlighted when people search for it [9]. Then give each service a matching page on your site; that pairing is our practice, not a Google rule.

Hours. List the hours you actually see clients; Google's guidelines say businesses that operate only by appointment shouldn't provide hours [32]. Whitespark's experts rank "business is open at time of search" fifth among local pack factors, and the report is blunt: "do NOT list your hours other than what they actually are" [6].

Photos. Google's photo tips ask for images with no significant alterations and no excessive use of filters or AI, saying "the image should represent reality" [33]. Heavily retouched results sit badly with that guidance. Show the clinic, the rooms and the team as they are.

Posts and questions. Posts older than six months are archived unless a date range is set [34], and Whitespark's experts rate the number of posts a ranking myth [6]. Google discontinued the Business Profile Q&A API on November 3, 2025 [35], and on March 12, 2026 began rolling out Ask Maps, a conversational feature in Google Maps, in the US and India [36]. Google said at launch that Ask Maps draws on more than 300 million places and reviews from more than 500 million contributors, so what your profile and reviews say is what it has to work with [36].

Measure it. Check the Performance report's search terms every month [15], and keep a matching Bing Places listing: Microsoft tells local businesses to register with Bing Places so address, hours and contact details stay eligible for AI answers [37]. Reviews, photos and before-and-after rules

Direct answer

Ask every client for an honest review through a link or QR code, and never offer anything in return: Google bans review incentives and asking only happy clients [38]. Treat before-and-after photos as advertising. The FTC's guides say such photos likely imply typical results [39], Meta limits these ads to adult audiences [40], and patient photos can raise privacy duties.

Reviews

Reviews move the map. Google's local ranking page says more reviews and positive ratings "can help your business's local ranking" [5].

Stat

In BrightLocal's 2026 survey of 1,002 US adults, 68% said they would only use a business rated four stars or higher, up from 55% in 2025, and 74% look for reviews written in the last three months [41].

Stat

In Tebra's 2025 survey of 3,964 US adults about healthcare providers of all kinds, 79% said they read reviews before choosing a provider [42].

Recent reviews may count for more than old ones. Sterling Sky and Places Scout analyzed 8,186 businesses in 200 US cities and found reviews gained in the current month mattered more than total reviews for "near me" rankings; the study is correlational [43].

The rules on asking are specific:

  • Google bans incentives such as payment, discounts, or free goods or services for any review, and bans discouraging negative reviews or asking only happy customers [38].
  • Google also bars pressuring customers to review while on your premises, staff review quotas, and requests for specific content, including content that names a staff member [38]. For an injector-led practice, that means no requests to name the injector.
  • Google allows asking for honest reviews: it suggests sending customers a link or a QR code [44].
  • The FTC's rule on consumer reviews, in force since October 21, 2024, bans fake reviews, incentives conditioned on a positive or negative review, undisclosed reviews by officers and managers, and review suppression through unfounded legal threats or intimidation [45][46]. FTC staff say the rule does not ban incentives with no sentiment condition, but Google's policy bans every review incentive on Google [46][38].

Replies need the same care as your charts. HHS's Office for Civil Rights has settled with healthcare practices over patient information disclosed in replies to online reviews: a New Jersey psychiatric practice paid $30,000 [47], and a California dental practice paid $23,000 [48]. The American Dental Association's model reply, written for dentists, shows the safe shape: broad "all patient" terms, and never a reference to a specific person or incident [49]. Whether HIPAA applies to your practice is a question for your counsel; the reply habit is worth keeping either way. Our guide on how to get Google reviews covers the asking.

Before-and-after photos

Before-and-after images are the most persuasive thing a med spa can show, and the most regulated. What the rules say depends on where the photo appears and who you are:

Where it appearsWhat the rule saysWho it binds
Your website and social postsBefore-and-after pictures are likely to convey that the result shown is what people will generally achieve [39]Advertisers, under the FTC's Endorsement Guides (guidance under the FTC Act, not a rule)
Meta adsAds for cosmetic procedures that depict before-and-after transformations are allowed only when targeting people 18 or older, and may not make statements of inferiority about physical appearance [40]Advertisers on Facebook and Instagram
Google AdsHealth is a sensitive category that covers invasive procedures, including injections, and advertiser-curated audiences can't be used [50]Google advertisers
Any marketing, where HIPAA appliesFull-face photographs are among HIPAA's identifiers [51], and marketing uses of protected health information need the patient's authorization, with narrow exceptions [52]HIPAA covered entities
Your Business ProfilePhotos should have no significant alterations and no excessive filters or AI [33]Profile owners, under Google's photo guidance

Two points need care. The link between HIPAA's photo identifiers and its marketing rule is a reasonable reading, but we found no HHS statement applying them to before-and-after galleries, so ask your counsel how they apply to yours. And Meta's policy text changes; the version cited here took effect July 22, 2026 [40].

Plastic surgeons who belong to ASPS work under a stricter written standard: the ASPS Code treats before-and-after images as unethical when they "use different light, poses, or photographic techniques" to misrepresent the results [53]. It binds ASPS members, not med spas, but it is a sound bar for any gallery: same light, same angle, same pose, same distance.

Key takeaway

Treat every before-and-after image as an ad: consent on file, consistent photography, and honesty about what is typical.

Checklist: reviews and photos

Local links, partnerships and citations

Direct answer

Earn mentions and links through real local relationships: press coverage, local "best of" lists, events, professional associations and partner businesses. Keep your name, address and phone identical everywhere they appear. Whitespark's experts give citations only 6% of the weight for map rankings but 13% for AI search visibility, so accurate listings now do two jobs [6].

Links still count. Google's local ranking page says prominence draws partly on how many websites link to your business [5], and Google's description of how Search ranks results names links from other prominent websites as one sign of quality [54].

Whitespark's experts put most of the AI-visibility weight off your site. Their top four AI search visibility factors were presence on expert-curated "best of" lists, a dedicated page for each service, prominence on key industry domains, and unstructured citations such as newspaper articles, blog posts, government sites and industry associations [6]. Listings carry extra weight in healthcare: Yext, which sells listings management, analyzed 6.8 million citations in AI answers and found listings made up 52.6% of the citations in healthcare [55].

Ideas that earn genuine links and mentions:

  1. Local press. A new treatment, a community event or an expert comment gives a local outlet a reason to write.
  2. Partners who share your clients. Salons, bridal shops and fitness studios can describe the partnership on their own sites.
  3. Associations. If a professional or trade association you belong to lists members, keep your listing complete and matching your profile.
  4. Sponsorships, marked honestly. Google's spam policy lists buying or selling links for ranking purposes as link spam, but paid links are fine when marked with rel="nofollow" or rel="sponsored" [28].

Consistency is the other half. Whitespark's experts count the absence of a crawlable name, address and phone on your website as a negative factor, and a mismatch in name, address or tracking numbers across the wider data ecosystem as another [6]. Keep your website, your profile, Bing Places and the major directories identical. Our local SEO guide covers citations step by step.

AI answers for aesthetic treatments

Direct answer

Google says its AI answers need no special optimization: a page must be indexed and eligible to show a snippet, and Business Profile information can help a business appear [56][8]. AI answers show only for some searches, and more often for questions than for short phrases [57]. Clear treatment pages and a complete profile give them something accurate to use.

Google's guidance is plain. Its AI features page says: "There are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, nor other special optimizations necessary" [56]. Its generative AI guide says: "Where appropriate, generative AI responses can include product listings, product information, and information about local businesses" [8]. The same guide says Google Business Profiles "can help your products and services to be visible in both AI responses and other Google Search results" [8].

AI answers are uneven. Pew's analysis of browsing data from 900 US adults in March 2025 found an AI summary on 18% of Google searches, on 8% of one- or two-word searches, and on 60% of searches that began with a question word [57]. A bare treatment name is the short kind; "how long does lip filler last" is the question kind. In our Las Vegas check, "botox" did show an AI Overview, and it cited sources such as MedlinePlus, WebMD and Drugs.com while naming none of the three businesses in the map results [1].

That is where treatment pages earn their keep. Google's generative AI guide says it can be helpful to have a unique viewpoint that stands out, because its AI systems look at a variety of sources [8]. In the peer-reviewed GEO study, adding citations, quotations and statistics raised visibility in AI answers by 30-40% on one measure; that was a lab test on a GPT-3.5 engine, not a promise [58].

People are using AI to choose. BrightLocal's 2026 survey found 45% of consumers had used AI tools for local business recommendations, up from 6% a year earlier; ChatGPT was the tool most used for business recommendations (31%), followed by Google's AI Mode (23%) [59]. Most check the answer: 88% of AI users said they fact-check the reviews an AI tool cites [59]. And AI tools are choosy: SOCi's study of more than 350,000 locations of multi-location brands found ChatGPT recommended just 1.2% of them, against an average 35.9% appearance rate in Google's local 3-Pack, and the locations it recommended averaged 4.3-star ratings [60].

Google also says its AI Overviews "can and will make mistakes" [61]. Search your main treatments from your own area every month and read what the AI answer says. If it gets your services wrong, fix the source: your pages and your profile. Skip the shortcuts, too: Google says you don't need AI text files, special markup or special schema to appear in its AI features, and that Google Search ignores llms.txt files [56][8].

One boundary: the AI place in our first page guarantee is Google's own AI answer on the results page, not ChatGPT or other assistants, and the written terms define how that share is measured. Our guide to AI search optimization covers the other platforms.

Ten marketing ideas that also build visibility

Direct answer

The best med spa marketing ideas do two jobs: they bring clients in this month and leave something that ranks next year. Treatment pages, a complete profile, a steady review habit, honest galleries, local partnerships and a monthly measurement routine all compound. Paid ads can fill the gaps while those build, within Google's and Meta's health-ad rules.

  1. One page per treatment, then one per real question. Recovery, how long results last, what affects price: each answers a search and gives AI answers something to cite.
  2. Providers on the page. A bio for each injector and the supervising physician supports the trust Google weighs most on health topics [20].
  3. A mirrored service list. One profile service for each treatment page [9].
  4. A review habit, not a review push. One link for every client, with nothing in return [38]. Whitespark's experts list a sustained influx of reviews over time among local pack factors, and too many reviews too fast among suspension risks [6].
  5. An honest gallery. Consent on file, consistent light and pose, and results you would call typical [39].
  6. A price page. A clear explanation of how price is set answers the "cost" searches on your list.
  7. Events. Open evenings and education nights earn event listings, press and partner mentions.
  8. Local partners. Businesses that serve the same clients can describe the partnership on their sites.
  9. Email to past clients. Where HIPAA applies, its marketing rule requires the patient's authorization before protected health information is used for marketing, with narrow exceptions; ask your counsel how that applies to your list [52].
  10. A monthly measurement routine. Check the profile's search terms [15], your Search Console queries [16], Bing Webmaster Tools' AI citation report, available since February 10, 2026 [37], and ChatGPT referrals, which carry utm_source=chatgpt.com [62].

On paid search, two health rules shape Google Ads for a med spa. Prescription drug terms such as botox need certification before a US campaign can keyword-target them [19], and injections fall in Google's sensitive health category, which rules out advertiser-curated audiences [50]. Be wary of med spa cost-per-lead benchmarks: LocaliQ's healthcare search benchmarks have no med spa row, and its dermatology figures are not a med spa benchmark [63].

Frequently asked questions

What is the best marketing for a med spa?

There is no single best channel, but the marketing that keeps paying back is the kind that ranks: one page per treatment, a complete Business Profile and a steady flow of genuine reviews. Ads and boosted posts stop when the budget does; a strong treatment page keeps working. Start by measuring the med spas already on page one for your treatments, then close the biggest gaps first.

How do med spas get more clients from Google?

By showing up where clients look: the map results for treatment searches near them, the organic results for longer questions, and Google's AI answers where they appear. The profile wins the map through categories, services, reviews and distance, and the website wins organic results with strong treatment pages. Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking [5].

Can med spas post before-and-after photos?

The rules depend on where the photo appears and who you are. The FTC's guides say before-and-after pictures are likely to be read as typical results [39]. Meta allows before-and-after cosmetic ads only when they target people 18 and over [40]. HIPAA lists full-face photographs among its identifiers [51]. Ask your counsel how these rules apply to your practice before you publish.

Do med spas show up in AI Overviews?

Sometimes. Google says its AI answers can include information about local businesses, and that Business Profiles can help [8]. But AI Overviews show only for some searches: Pew found AI summaries on 18% of Google searches in March 2025, and far more often for questions [57]. In our Las Vegas check, "botox" showed one, and it named none of the businesses in the map results [1].

How long does it take a med spa to reach page one?

It depends on the gap between you and the med spas already there. Google's starter guide says some changes take effect in a few hours and others take several months, so wait a few weeks before judging [64]. In Ahrefs' 2025 study, 40.82% of pages that reached the top 10 did so within a month, yet only 1.74% of a random sample of new pages got there within a year [65].

Which Business Profile category should a med spa use?

Choose the most specific category that completes Google's test "this business IS a" [32]. For a business that is a medical spa, that category is Medical spa, which appears on PlePer's list of US categories [30]. Add only a few more that also describe the whole business, such as Skin care clinic, where that is a core line. Google says categories affect local ranking, and editing them can trigger re-verification [29].
