How Plastic Surgery Practices Reach Page One: Marketing Strategies

How plastic surgery practices reach page one: procedure pages built on the ASPS 2025 figures, accurate surgeon credentials, before-and-after galleries that meet the ASPS Code and ad rules, a complete profile, reviews and AI answers.

Plastic surgery practices reach page one with strong procedure pages, accurate surgeon credentials, a complete Google Business Profile and a steady flow of genuine reviews, built on a measurement of the practices already there. The marketing is tightly bound by rules: the ASPS Code of Ethics on photos, promised outcomes and "board certified" claims, the FTC's guides on endorsements, and Google's and Meta's health-ad policies.

This guide covers what page one holds for cosmetic procedure searches, how to measure your competition, and the strategies that build lasting visibility. Where a rule applies, we say what it says, who it binds and when it took effect, and leave how it applies to your practice to your own counsel.

What page one looks like for cosmetic procedure searches

Direct answer

Some cosmetic procedure searches are won almost entirely in the organic results, with the map results and AI answers varying by phrase. In our Las Vegas check, "plastic surgeon" showed no map results and no AI answer, and most of its organic results were surgeon practice websites [1]. Strong procedure pages are the core of plastic surgery marketing.

We searched 12 commercial phrases from Las Vegas on September 27, 2026, on desktop, and recorded what page one held [1]. "Plastic surgeon" was the only one of the 12 with no map results [1]. Its organic results held surgeon practice websites, Yelp, and a page on find.plasticsurgery.org, the ASPS domain [1]. One capture is a snapshot, not a rule; other phrases, such as a procedure plus "near me", may show map results. That is why you measure every phrase, in every place, from your own market.

The demand is growing. The American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS) published its 2025 figures on September 1, 2026:

Stat

ASPS estimates 1,523,330 cosmetic surgical procedures in 2025, up 7% from 1,425,390 in 2024 [2].

Stat

The top five were liposuction (317,196), breast augmentation and implant revision (304,234, up 11%), tummy tuck (173,251), breast lift (156,131) and eyelid surgery (118,978, up nearly 19%) [2][3].

Patients aged 66 and over saw a 24% rise in procedure volume, the largest gain of any age group [3]. ASPS says its figures come from member surgeons, its endorsed partner CosmetAssure and a national claims-based dataset, together representing more than 3,000 board-certified plastic surgeons, statistically extrapolated [2]. For minimally invasive treatments, ASPS reports rankings by volume rather than procedure counts: neuromodulator injections, commonly referred to as Botox, made up roughly half, and hyaluronic acid fillers another 30% [2].

Key takeaway

Measure every phrase in each place separately; for some cosmetic searches Google shows no map results at all, and only your pages can reach page one.

Measure the practices already on page one

Direct answer

Before you spend on marketing, measure the practices already on page one for your procedures. For organic results, record what each ranking page covers and how many sites link to it. For the map results, record categories, reviews and ratings. For AI answers, record which pages Google cites. The gap between you and them is your plan.

Google's own advice on hiring an SEO tells business owners to ask what results to expect, in what timeframe, and how success will be measured [4]. It is also blunt about promises:

We agree with it. Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking [5], and that third-party SEO tools don't have access to its internal ranking data [4]. What you can measure is the gap:

Because the organic results carry so much weight in this field, two gap reports earn their keep: a content gap, which lists keywords competitors rank for and you don't [9], and a link gap, which lists sites that link to competitors but not to you [10]. Keyword difficulty scores from these tools are estimates built from the links of the pages already ranking, not Google's own data [6][4]. A simple way to think about it: for each phrase, find the weakest practice or page holding a slot in each place, measure how far you are behind it, and work the smallest gaps first. The phrase list: procedures x modifiers x places

Direct answer

Build the phrase list from three columns: the procedures you perform, the words patients add (cost, before and after, recovery, surgeon, revision, near me), and the places you draw patients from. Check it against autocomplete, Keyword Planner, your profile's search terms and Search Console. Keep every phrase a real patient would type, including the small ones.

ProceduresModifiersPlaces
liposuction, breast augmentation, implant revision, tummy tuck, breast lift, eyelid surgery, facelift, rhinoplastycost, before and after, recovery, surgeon, revision, financing, reviews, near meyour city, its neighborhoods, the region patients travel from

Let the ASPS figures shape priorities. The five most common procedures of 2025 and the fastest-growing age group, patients 66 and over, point to where demand is moving [2][3]. Then check the list against four sources of real searches:

  1. Autocomplete. Google says its predictions reflect real searches and vary with the location a query comes from [11].
  2. Keyword Planner. Its volumes are averages over close variants, rounded, and tied to the location you choose [12].
  3. Your Business Profile. The Performance report lists the search terms people used to find your profile, updated at the start of each month [13].
  4. Search Console. Its queries dimension groups your site's data by the searches people typed [14].

Keep the small phrases. In Ahrefs' US database, almost 93% of keywords get fewer than 10 searches a month [15]. A phrase like a procedure plus "revision" plus your city may show no volume and still be the search of a patient ready to book.

Procedure pages and surgeon credentials

Direct answer

Give each procedure its own page: who it suits, how the surgery is done, recovery, risks, typical results and how cost is set, with the operating surgeon's credentials stated exactly. In Whitespark's 2026 survey, a dedicated page for each service was the top factor for local organic rankings and the second for AI search visibility [7].

Surgery is squarely the kind of topic where Google raises the bar. Google says: "our systems give even more weight to content that aligns with strong E-E-A-T for topics that could significantly impact the health, financial stability, or safety of people" [16]. E-E-A-T stands for experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust, and Google says trust matters most [16]. Google's starter guide is also clear that E-E-A-T is not itself a ranking factor [17]. Google encourages accurate bylines where readers might expect them, which for a procedure page means the surgeon [16].

Credentials have their own rules:

  • The ASPS Code treats a claim of board-certified specialist status as unethical unless the member is certified by a board recognized by the American Board of Medical Specialties, or its Canadian or Quebec equivalents [18]. The code, updated January 2025, binds ASPS members.
  • California law bars physicians from claiming board certification unless the board is an American Board of Medical Specialties member board, a board with equivalent requirements approved by the physician's licensing board before a set date, or one with an ACGME-approved postgraduate training program; the term "board certified" also needs the full name of the certifying board with comparable prominence [19].

Name the board in full, every time. The code also treats as unethical any communication that shows results "not typical of the results obtained by the average patient" without clearly disclosing that fact, or that "contains a guarantee that satisfaction or a cure will result" [18]. Keep clinical claims and any first page ranking promise far apart: one is about surgery, the other about search results.

Claims need evidence. The FTC's health products guidance says advertisers should not make claims through testimonials or expert endorsements that would be deceptive, or couldn't be substantiated, if the advertiser made them directly [20]. It is written for health products, so ask your counsel how it applies to claims about procedures.

Build depth, not duplicates. Google's spam policies treat pages created to rank for similar searches that funnel visitors to one page as doorway abuse, and pages generated at scale to manipulate rankings as scaled content abuse [21]. One thorough page per procedure beats a thin copy for every nearby town. Our organic SEO guide covers on-page work in depth.

Before-and-after galleries: consent and advertising rules

Direct answer

A before-and-after gallery is advertising, and several rules apply at once. The ASPS Code bars photos that use different lighting, poses or techniques to misrepresent results [18]. The FTC's guides say such photos likely imply typical results [22]. Meta limits these ads to adults [23], and patient photos can raise HIPAA duties [24]. Consent, consistent photography and honest labeling are the working standard.

The ASPS Code treats before-and-after images as unethical when they "use different light, poses, or photographic techniques" to misrepresent the results achieved [18]. The FTC's Endorsement Guides give their own example: an ad that simply features before and after pictures "is likely to convey that the endorser's experience is representative" of what people will generally achieve [22].

Two points need care. The link between HIPAA's photo identifiers and its marketing rule is a reasonable reading, but we found no HHS statement applying them to before-and-after galleries, so ask your counsel how they apply to yours. And Meta's policy text changes; the version cited here took effect July 22, 2026 [23].

Under the code, the agency does not carry the risk for you. The ASPS Code says a member "shall be held personally responsible" for any code violation by a public relations, advertising or similar firm the member retains [18]. Paid endorsements are allowed under the code only if the compensation is clearly and conspicuously disclosed [18]. Read every ad, gallery caption and influencer post your agency drafts as if it carried your name, because under the code it does.

Key takeaway

Under the ASPS Code, the surgeon answers for the agency's code violations, so every gallery needs consent on file, consistent photography and honest labels.

The Business Profile for a surgical practice

Direct answer

A complete Business Profile lets you compete in the map results wherever Google shows them. Choose Plastic surgeon or Plastic surgery clinic as the primary category, list each procedure as a service, use your real-world name, keep hours real and follow Google's rules for practitioner profiles [27][28]. Google says complete, accurate profiles are more likely to show in local results [5].

Categories. Google publishes no public category list, so marketers rely on compiled ones. PlePer's US list includes Plastic surgeon, Plastic surgery clinic and Cosmetic surgeon [27]. A profile has one primary category and up to nine more, and the first field is the primary [29]. Google's guidelines say to choose the fewest categories that describe your core business, to pick ones that complete the statement "This business IS a", and never to use categories as keywords [28]. Google says the categories you choose affect your local ranking, and changing them can trigger re-verification [30].

Surgeons and the practice. Google's guidelines say doctors are individual practitioners, and that a practitioner's profile may include a title or degree such as "Dr." or "MD" [28]. For a solo practitioner at a branded practice, the guidelines call for one profile named in the form "[brand]: [practitioner name]" [28]. Check the guidelines before adding profiles at one address: Whitespark's experts rank a same-category profile at the same address among the strongest negative factors [7].

Name, services and hours. Google's guidelines say adding unnecessary information to the business name can get a profile suspended [28]. Google says businesses can add custom services with details such as price and description, and that a listed service may be highlighted when people search for it [31]. List the hours you actually see patients; Google's guidelines say businesses that operate only by appointment shouldn't provide hours [28].

Photos. Google's photo tips ask for images with no significant alterations and no excessive use of filters or AI, saying "the image should represent reality" [32]. Show the practice, the team and the consultation rooms.

Measure it. Check the Performance report's search terms every month [13]. Our Google Business Profile optimization guide covers every field.

Reviews and reputation

Direct answer

Many patients weigh reviews more heavily for specialists: in Tebra's 2025 survey, 51% said reviews matter more when choosing a specialist than a primary care provider [33]. Ask every patient for an honest review through a link or QR code, never offer anything in return, and reply without confirming anyone is a patient [34].

Stat

In Tebra's 2025 survey of 803 patients, 62% had avoided booking with a provider because of negative reviews, and Google reviews ranked highest in influence, at 83% [33].

Stat

Press Ganey found people read 4.7 reviews on average before deciding on a healthcare provider, and most consider 4 out of 5 stars the lowest acceptable rating [35].

Replies matter to patients. In the same Tebra study, 45% of providers said they never respond to negative reviews, while 71% of patients said they trust providers more when they do [33]. Tebra and Press Ganey both sell to practices, so read their figures as vendor studies. Google says more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking [5].

The rules on asking are specific. Google bans incentives such as payment, discounts, or free goods or services for any review, bans asking only happy customers, and bars on-site pressure, staff review quotas and requests for specific content, including content that names a staff member [34]. Google suggests sending customers a link or a QR code [36]. The FTC's rule on consumer reviews, in force since October 21, 2024, bans fake reviews, incentives conditioned on a positive or negative review, undisclosed reviews by officers and managers, and suppressing reviews through unfounded legal threats, intimidation or public false accusations [37][38].

Replies carry privacy risk. HHS's Office for Civil Rights has settled with healthcare practices over patient information disclosed in replies to online reviews: a New Jersey psychiatric practice paid $30,000 [39], and a California dental practice paid $23,000 [40]. The American Dental Association's model reply, written for dentists, shows the safe shape: broad "all patient" terms, and never a reference to a specific person or incident [41]. Whether HIPAA applies to your practice is a question for your counsel; the habit is worth keeping either way. Our guide on how to get Google reviews covers the asking.

AI answers for procedure questions

Direct answer

Many patients research surgery with questions, and questions are where AI answers appear most. Google says its AI answers need no special optimization and can include local businesses, helped by an accurate Business Profile [42][8]. Thorough procedure pages that answer real questions, with sources and the surgeon's name, give AI answers something accurate to cite.

Pew's analysis of browsing data from 900 US adults in March 2025 found an AI summary on 18% of Google searches, on 8% of one- or two-word searches, and on 60% of searches that began with a question word [43]. "Plastic surgeon" showed none in our Las Vegas check [1]. A question such as "how long is tummy tuck recovery" is the kind of search far more likely to show one [43].

Google's guidance is plain. Its AI features page says: "There are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, nor other special optimizations necessary" [42]. Its generative AI guide says: "Where appropriate, generative AI responses can include product listings, product information, and information about local businesses" [8]. The same guide says it can be helpful to have a unique viewpoint that stands out, because its AI systems look at a variety of sources [8].

What earns citations? In the peer-reviewed GEO study, adding citations, quotations and statistics raised visibility in AI answers by 30-40% on one measure; that was a lab test on a GPT-3.5 engine, not a promise [44]. In Ahrefs' study of 75K brands, web mentions correlated with AI Overview visibility at 0.664, far more strongly than backlinks at 0.218; correlation is not cause [45]. Press coverage, association listings and patient-education appearances all build mentions.

People use these tools to choose. BrightLocal's 2026 survey found 45% of consumers had used AI tools for local business recommendations, up from 6% a year earlier, and 88% of AI users said they fact-check the reviews an AI tool cites [46]. Google also says its AI Overviews "can and will make mistakes" [47]. Search your main procedures each month and correct your own pages where the AI answer gets you wrong. Skip the shortcuts: Google says you don't need AI text files or special markup, and that Google Search ignores llms.txt files [42][8].

One boundary: the AI place in our first page guarantee is Google's own AI answer on the results page, not ChatGPT or other assistants, and the written terms define how that share is measured. Our guide to AI search optimization covers the other platforms.

Paid search and social: the rules and the numbers

Direct answer

Paid ads can fill gaps while organic work builds, within tight rules. Google treats cosmetic surgery as a sensitive health category, so advertiser-curated audiences such as Customer Match can't be used [25]. Meta allows before-and-after cosmetic ads only when they target adults [23]. LocaliQ's 2024-25 medians put plastic surgery search leads at $102.51 each [48].

Stat

LocaliQ's medians for plastic and cosmetic surgery search ads, from a sample of 3,542 US healthcare search campaigns running October 1, 2024 to September 30, 2025, were a 5.81% click-through rate, $5.75 per click, an 8.52% conversion rate and $102.51 per lead [48].

LocaliQ reports that the conversion rate was up 38% and cost per lead down 29% year on year [48]. These are medians from LocaliQ's own client campaigns, so compare them with your own numbers rather than treating them as a target. On Google, health is a sensitive category that covers "invasive medical procedures, including cosmetic surgery", and advertiser-curated audiences, such as Customer Match and your data segments, can't be used [25]. On Meta, before-and-after cosmetic ads are allowed only when targeting people 18 or older [23]. And under the ASPS Code, the surgeon is responsible for the advertising firm's code violations [18].

A plastic surgery marketing checklist

Direct answer

Plastic surgery marketing that lasts runs as one system: a measured phrase list, a thorough page per procedure, credentials stated exactly, an honest gallery, a complete profile, a steady flow of genuine reviews and a monthly check of what Google shows. Paid ads fill gaps within Google's and Meta's health rules, and every ad goes past the surgeon first.

Frequently asked questions

How do plastic surgeons get more patients online?

By winning the places patients look: the organic results for procedure searches, the map results where Google shows them, and AI answers to procedure questions. In our Las Vegas check, "plastic surgeon" showed no map results and no AI answer, and most of its organic results were surgeon websites [1]. Thorough procedure pages, exact credentials, an honest gallery and genuine reviews do the work. Measure the leading practices first.

Can plastic surgeons post before-and-after photos?

Several rules apply, depending on who you are and where the photo runs. The ASPS Code bars photos that use different lighting, poses or techniques to misrepresent results [18]. The FTC's guides say such photos are likely read as typical results [22]. Meta allows these ads only when they target adults [23]. HIPAA lists full-face photographs among its identifiers [24]. Ask your counsel before you publish.

How important are reviews for plastic surgeons?

Very. In Tebra's 2025 survey, 51% of patients said reviews matter more when choosing a specialist than a primary care provider, and 62% had avoided a provider because of negative reviews [33]. Google also says more reviews and positive ratings can help local ranking [5]. Build a steady flow from real patients, and reply without confirming anyone is a patient.

Do plastic surgery practices appear in AI Overviews?

Sometimes. Google says its AI answers can include information about local businesses [8]. But AI Overviews show only for some searches: Pew found AI summaries on 18% of Google searches in March 2025, and on 60% of searches beginning with a question word [43]. Procedure questions are where they appear most, so answer those questions thoroughly on your own pages.

How long does it take to reach page one for procedure searches?

It depends on the gap between you and the practices already there. Google's starter guide says some changes take effect in a few hours and others take several months, so wait a few weeks before judging [17]. In Ahrefs' 2025 study, 40.82% of pages that reached the top 10 did so within a month, yet only 1.74% of a random sample of new pages got there within a year [49].

Should each procedure have its own page?

Yes. In Whitespark's 2026 survey of local search experts, a dedicated page for each service was the top factor for local organic rankings and the second for AI search visibility [7]. Google advises putting the words people search for in the title and main heading [50]. Make each page thorough and distinct; Google's spam policies target near-duplicate pages built only to rank [21].
