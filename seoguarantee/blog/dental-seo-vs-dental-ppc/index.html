SEO vs PPC for Dentists: Cost, Timing and When to Use Both

How dental SEO and PPC compare on cost, timing and what lasts, with LocaliQ's dental ad benchmarks, Google's health ad rules, Local Services Ads and a plan for using both.

How SEO works for a dental practice

Direct answer

SEO earns unpaid places in three parts of Google's results: the map results through your Google Business Profile, the organic results, and Google's AI answers. Google says it "never accepts money to include or rank sites in our search results", and that there is no way to pay for a better local ranking. Relevance, reviews, pages and links decide who shows up.

The organic clicks concentrate near the top of the page. In Advanced Web Ranking's July 2026 data for US desktop searches, the first organic result averaged a 20.02% click-through rate, the second 10.36% and the third 3.89%, and the first three results took roughly 92% of the clicks that reached the top ten [7]. Health searches earn fewer organic clicks than most: across all US devices in July 2026, AWR measured 13.3% for the first result in Health & Fitness, near the bottom of its 12.2% to 31.2% range by industry [7].

AI answers take a share too. AWR found AI Overviews on 43% of the desktop results pages it sampled in July 2026, cutting the first organic result's click-through rate by 52%, from 20.02% to 9.69% [7]. Google, for its part, said in August 2025 that total organic click volume had been "relatively stable" year over year, and called third-party reports of dramatic declines inaccurate [8]. Our article on organic click-through rate covers the studies side by side.

How PPC works: Google Ads and Local Services Ads

Direct answer

With Google Ads, a practice bids on search phrases and pays when someone clicks its ad, for as long as the budget lasts. Local Services Ads are a separate Google product, ranked in an auction on bid and profile quality, which Google says includes your rating, number of reviews, response time and completed verification checks. Neither buys a better place in the unpaid results.

Local Services Ads have their own entry gate. Google says every business must pass its screening and verification process, which "varies by business category and location" [9], so check whether the ads are offered for your practice in your area. Since October 20, 2025, one Google Verified badge has replaced Google Guaranteed and the other Local Services Ads badges [10][11]. Our article on Google Guaranteed explains the change.

Paid placements are spreading on local searches. Sterling Sky's tracking through 2025 found ads in the local pack on almost 22% of its mobile reports, up from 1%, and Local Services Ads on 31% of tracked queries by November, up from roughly 11% [12]. Ads still don't lift your unpaid rankings: Whitespark's 2026 survey of 47 local search experts rates taking part in Google Ads or other paid Google products a myth as a map ranking factor [13].

What dental ads cost

Direct answer

LocaliQ's benchmarks, drawn from 3,542 US search campaigns run between October 1, 2024 and September 30, 2025, put the median cost per click for general dentistry at $7.03 and the median cost per lead at $84.77. Emergency dentistry, oral surgery and orthodontics ran $7.85 to $8.76 per click. These are medians from LocaliQ's own client campaigns, so your market may differ.

Budget from the cost per lead, not the cost per click, and track which leads become booked patients. We found no comparable public benchmark for SEO pricing, so compare SEO proposals on what they measure and promise in writing, not on a price per click. Google's own line is that "it costs nothing to appear in our organic search results" [3].

The health ad rules that shape dental campaigns

Direct answer

Google treats health as a sensitive category for personalized ads, covering "Invasive medical procedures, including cosmetic surgery, surgical procedures, or injections", and says advertiser-curated audiences such as Customer Match can't be used there. In the US, keyword-targeting prescription drug terms such as Botox requires certification. Ads exclusively for dental insurance coverage aren't restricted.

The sources: Google's health personalized-advertising policy [14], its restricted drug terms policy, which says you "must be certified in order to keyword-target these terms" [15], its prescription drugs list, which includes botox [16], and its healthcare and medicines policy [17]. We found nothing in Google's policies saying whether dental implant or oral surgery ads count as "surgical procedures", so confirm that before building remarketing or customer-list audiences for them.

Conversion tracking needs the same care as the site. HHS's tracking guidance says disclosing protected health information to tracking vendors for marketing without HIPAA-compliant authorizations "would constitute impermissible disclosures" [18]. Keep ad pixels off booking pages until counsel has reviewed the setup; our guide to HIPAA marketing rules has the detail.

The ad copy has rules of its own. The ADA Code, 2025 edition, binds ADA members: it bars advertising that is false or misleading "in any material respect", asks general dentists not to imply specialization, and calls paying a marketing vendor "a specific amount or percentage of professional fees collected" fee splitting (4.E.1) [19]. That last point matters for pay-per-patient ad deals; take any such offer to counsel or your board first. State rules apply too. Florida, for example, requires ads for free or discounted services by dentists and several other practitioners to carry a statement of the patient's right to refuse to pay, cancel payment or be reimbursed for any other service performed as a result of, and within 72 hours of, responding to the ad [20].

Timing, and what you keep

Direct answer

Ads run while the budget runs; SEO builds over months. Google says some changes take effect in a few hours and others take several months, and suggests waiting a few weeks to judge. Until June 2026, its hiring guidance said results typically took four months to a year. When the ad budget stops, the ads stop; SEO results can hold, but are never fixed.

Ahrefs' timing study shows why patience and measurement both matter. Of a million random new URLs, 1.74% reached the top 10 within a year, and 40.82% of the pages that did get there did so within a month [21]. Those were random pages with no targeted work, so read them as proof that page one is not automatic, not as a forecast for a managed campaign. Google also says positions in its results "aren't static or fixed in place" [6]. For the full picture, see how long SEO takes.

When to use each, and how to use both

Direct answer

Use PPC when you need bookings for a treatment this month, when a new practice has no rankings yet, or to test which phrases convert. Use SEO for the treatments and places you'll serve for years, and for the unpaid map and organic results that no one can buy. Run together, ad data shows which phrases book patients, and SEO builds unpaid visibility for those phrases.

  • PPC first: a new practice or location, a treatment with open chairs this month, or phrases where AI answers and ads leave few organic clicks.
  • SEO first: your core treatments, the map results, and the reviews and pages that also feed AI answers.
  • Both: reviews count toward Local Services Ads profile quality [2] and, Google says, can help your local ranking [4].

Frequently asked questions

Is SEO or PPC better for dentists?

Neither wins everywhere. PPC buys visibility for as long as you pay, at a median $7.03 per click for general dentistry in LocaliQ's benchmarks [1]. SEO earns unpaid places that Google says can't be bought [3][4], but it takes months. Use ads for bookings you need this month, SEO for the treatments you'll offer for years, and both where the budget allows.

How much do dental Google Ads cost?

In LocaliQ's benchmarks from 3,542 US search campaigns, October 2024 to September 2025, the median cost per click was $7.03 for general dentistry, $7.85 for emergency dentistry and oral surgery, and $8.76 for orthodontics. Median cost per lead ran from $55.15 for oral surgery to $84.77 for general dentistry [1]. These are LocaliQ client medians, so check them against your own market.

How long does SEO take compared with ads?

Ads run while the budget runs; SEO builds over months. Google says some changes take effect in hours and others take several months, and suggests waiting a few weeks to judge [5]. Until June 2026, its hiring guidance said results typically took four months to a year [22]. In Ahrefs' study, 40.82% of pages that reached the top 10 got there within a month [21].

Do Local Services Ads work for dentists?

They follow their own rules. Local Services Ads are ranked in an auction on bid and profile quality, which includes your rating, number of reviews and completed verification checks [2]. To join, a business must pass Google's screening, which varies by business category and location [9], so check availability for your practice. Since October 20, 2025, the ads carry a single Google Verified badge, which replaced Google Guaranteed [10][11].

Should a new practice start with ads or SEO?

Start both, in different roles. A new practice has no reviews or rankings yet, and in Ahrefs' study 72.9% of pages in Google's top ten were more than three years old [21]. Ads can bring early bookings while the Business Profile, reviews and treatment pages build. Shift budget toward SEO as the unpaid places arrive, and keep ads on the treatments where they pay.

Where SEO fits in a dental budget

SEO is the part of the budget that builds an asset, which is why we measure the competition before promising anything; see our dental SEO work and the Katy Dental Experts case study.
