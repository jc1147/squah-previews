Organic SEO is the work of earning unpaid places in Google's regular results with pages that Google can crawl, understand and trust more than the pages already there. No one can buy those places, and Google's own starter guide says useful content will likely do more for them than any other tip it gives [1][2]. This guide explains how ranking works in Google's words, the on-page, technical and link work that moves pages, the spam rules that sink them, what page one is worth, and how to measure progress honestly.

What organic SEO is, and what it is not

Direct answer

Organic SEO earns the unpaid listings in Google's regular results, the ones that sit apart from the ads and the local map results. Google says it never accepts money to include or rank sites there, and that appearing costs nothing [1]. What you control is whether your pages are the most relevant, useful and trusted answer to the searches your customers make.

A results page has several places a business can appear. Ads are paid. The map results come from Google Business Profiles and follow local ranking rules, covered in our local SEO guide and in local SEO vs organic SEO. Google's AI answers, where they appear, are grounded in pages its core ranking systems retrieve from the search index [3], so organic SEO is the groundwork for them too.

It is not a trick, and it is not a relationship with Google. Google's documentation says so plainly: "Google doesn't accept payment to crawl a site more frequently, or rank it higher. If anyone tells you otherwise, they're wrong." [4] The same page says Google doesn't promise to crawl, index or serve any page, even one that follows its rules [4].

How Google finds, indexes and ranks pages

Direct answer

Google works in three stages. It crawls the web to discover pages, indexes the pages it can understand, and serves results by ranking indexed pages for each search. Not every page passes every stage. Ranking is automated, works mainly page by page with some site-wide signals, and weighs hundreds of factors, including the searcher's location, language and device.

Google puts the first point in one line: "Google Search works in three stages, and not all pages make it through each stage" [4]. A page that is never crawled, or crawled but not indexed, cannot rank for anything, which is why technical SEO comes before polishing copy.

Once a page is indexed, Google's ranking systems decide where it shows. Google says: "Relevancy is determined by hundreds of factors, which could include information such as the user's location, language, and device (desktop or phone)." [4] Its ranking guide says the systems "are designed to work on the page level," and that site-wide signals and classifiers are also used [5]. In practice, each page competes on its own merits, and the site around it still counts.

Google's ranking systems guide names 17 systems in use today [5]. The ones that shape everyday SEO work:

SystemWhat Google says it does
BERT and neural matchingUnderstands how combinations of words express meaning, and matches concepts in queries to concepts in pages
FreshnessShows "fresher content for queries where it would be expected"
Link analysis and PageRankUses links to understand what pages are about; PageRank "continues to be part of our core ranking systems"
Original contentShows original reporting "ahead of those who merely cite it"
Reliable informationAims to "surface more authoritative pages and demote low-quality content"
ReviewsAims to "reward high quality reviews, content that provides insightful analysis and original research"
Site diversityGenerally shows no more than two listings from the same site near the top

The helpful content system is no longer on that list as a separate system. Google explains: "In March 2024, it evolved and became part of our core ranking systems, as our systems use a variety of signals and systems to present helpful results to users." [5] The March 2024 core update changed several core systems at once, and Google wrote: "There's no longer one signal or system used to do this" [6]. So there is no single helpfulness score to chase. Helpfulness is judged across the core systems.

Stat

Google reported 45% less low-quality, unoriginal content in its results after the March 2024 changes; the figure is self-reported, with no published method [7]

Those core systems change several times a year. Google says core updates are "broad in nature, and don't target specific sites or individual web pages" [8]. Its Search Status Dashboard lists confirmed core updates dated March 13, June 30 and December 11, 2025, and March 27 and May 21, 2026 [9]. Google's advice after a drop is patient: it says it can take several months for its systems to recognize improvements, sometimes not until the next core update, and that "positions in Google Search results aren't static or fixed in place" [8].

Key takeaway

No single signal decides a ranking; Google's systems weigh relevance, quality and usefulness page by page, and re-weigh them in several core updates a year.

What Google says about quality: E-E-A-T, raters and authority scores

Direct answer

E-E-A-T stands for experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust, and Google says trust is the most important of the four. Google also says E-E-A-T is not itself a ranking factor. Its systems do give more weight to content showing strong E-E-A-T on topics that could affect people's health, finances or safety, which includes much of what health practices publish.

Google's definition is short: "experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness, or what we call E-E-A-T. Of these aspects, trust is most important." [10] The "experience" part arrived in December 2022, when Google's quality rater guidelines gained the extra E [11].

It is not a score and not a ranking factor. Google's starter guide lists "Thinking E-E-A-T is a ranking factor" among its myths and answers: "No, it's not." [2] What Google does say is this: "While E-E-A-T itself isn't a specific ranking factor … our systems give even more weight to content that aligns with strong E-E-A-T for topics that could significantly impact the health, financial stability, or safety of people" [10]. Our reading: dental, med spa, plastic surgery and mental health pages sit at or near that category, so the bar for accuracy and sourcing is higher for them.

Quality raters don't move rankings either. Google says: "Search raters have no control over how pages rank. Rater data is not used directly in our ranking algorithms." [10] The current rater guidelines, dated September 11, 2025, say no single rating can directly change how a page appears in Search [12].

The authority numbers that SEO tools print are not Google's either. Google says third-party reputation or authority scores "don't correspond to any of Google's own signals nor come from Google." [6]

What to do with all this:

  • Put a real author on every article. Google says it strongly encourages "adding accurate authorship information, such as bylines to content where readers might expect it." [10]
  • Show first-hand experience: your own photos, process and prices.
  • Cite sources for health and cost claims.

On-page SEO: matching intent and covering the topic

Direct answer

On-page SEO makes each page the clearest match for one search intent. Put the words people search for in the title and main heading, cover the subtopics searchers expect, answer the question early and add what the ranking pages lack. Google calls compelling, useful content more likely to influence your presence in results than any other suggestion in its guide.

Start with Google's plainest instruction: "Use words that people would use to look for your content, and place those words in prominent locations on the page, such as the title and main heading of a page" [13]. Google's How Search Works pages explain why. The most basic relevance signal is a page containing the same keywords as the search, in its headings or body text, and Google's systems then look for other relevant content beyond the keyword itself [14]. A page about dental implants that never mentions cost, recovery or who is a good candidate covers less of the topic than one that does, whatever its word count.

Intent comes first. Sistrix's research on click-through rates found that a keyword's search intention defines the layout of its results page, and the layout decides how many organic clicks are available, so search volume alone is not the deciding figure [15]. If Google answers a search with a map and three businesses, a long article will struggle to win it; a strong service page and a strong profile have a better chance.

Google publishes the questions it wants creators to ask of their own pages [10]:

  • "Does the content provide original information, reporting, research, or analysis?"
  • "Does the content provide a substantial, complete, or comprehensive description of the topic?"
  • "If the content draws on other sources, does it avoid simply copying or rewriting those sources, and instead provide substantial additional value and originality?"

That last question is the honest meaning of "do everything they did and more." Cover what the pages on page one cover, then add what they lack: your prices, your process, your photos, your data. Copying them fails Google's own test.

Three myths to drop, all answered in Google's starter guide [2]:

  1. Meta keywords: "Google Search doesn't use the keywords meta tag."
  2. Word counts: "there's no magical word count target, minimum or maximum".
  3. Duplicate content penalties: "If you have some content that's accessible under multiple URLs, it's fine; don't fret about it."

Technical SEO: crawling, speed and page experience

Direct answer

Technical SEO makes sure Google can find, crawl and index your pages and that visitors get a fast, stable experience. Google uses Core Web Vitals in ranking and sets "good" at LCP within 2.5 seconds, INP under 200 milliseconds and CLS under 0.1. Relevance still wins, though: Google says it shows the most relevant content even when page experience is sub-par.

Crawling depends on links. Google says it uses links "to find new pages to crawl" [16], so a page that no other page links to is hard to discover. And indexing is never automatic: check which of your pages Google has actually indexed before you judge their rankings.

Speed and stability count, within limits. Google's page experience documentation says: "Core Web Vitals are used by our ranking systems." It adds: "Beyond Core Web Vitals, other page experience aspects don't directly help your website rank higher in search results." [17] Google's thresholds for a good score [18]:

Core Web VitalGoogle's threshold for good
Largest Contentful Paint (LCP)Within the first 2.5 seconds
Interaction to Next Paint (INP)Under 200 milliseconds
Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS)Under 0.1

Keep the scale in perspective. Google says: "Google Search always seeks to show the most relevant content, even if the page experience is sub-par." [17] It also says good results in Search Console's Core Web Vitals report or in other tools don't by themselves put a page at the top of the results [17]. Fix what is slow and unstable, then put the next hour into content.

Structured data is the other technical lever, and it is often oversold. Google says it "uses structured data that it finds on the web to understand the content of the page" [19], and markup can make a page eligible for rich results. Google's case studies report higher click-through rates on rich results, such as Nestlé's measurement of an 82% higher click-through rate for pages shown as rich results, a company-reported figure with no published method [19]. But Google doesn't promise your markup will show, and even a manual action against a page's structured data "doesn't affect how the page ranks in Google web search." [20] None of these Google pages describes markup as a ranking boost.

One more technical point. Google says its AI features need nothing extra: "You don't need to create new machine readable files, AI text files, or markup to appear in these features. There's also no special schema.org structured data that you need to add." [21] An llms.txt file or "AI schema" is not a Google ranking factor.

Off-page SEO: links and mentions that signal trust

Direct answer

Off-page SEO is mostly links and mentions from other websites. Google uses links to find pages and to judge relevance, and PageRank remains part of its core ranking systems, though Google calls it one signal among many. Links bought or built mainly to manipulate rankings break Google's spam policies. Links earned because your page is worth citing are the goal.

Google's link documentation says: "Google uses links as a signal when determining the relevancy of pages and to find new pages to crawl." [16] Its starter guide adds perspective: "We have many ranking signals, and PageRank is just one of those." [2] Google's How Search Works pages go further on quality, naming whether other prominent websites link to or refer to the content as one of the factors it uses, and calling that generally a good sign the information is trustworthy [14].

How many links does a page need? No one outside Google knows the formula, but you can measure the pages you are trying to pass. Ahrefs' rule of thumb is that to reach the top ten you will generally need as many backlinks as the pages already ranking there, "if not more", while noting that matching the leaders is no promise of the first result [22]. Ahrefs' KD estimates that bar from the referring domains of the current top ten, Semrush's KD% adds authority scores and features of the results page, and Ahrefs itself says no difficulty metric can accurately predict your chances of ranking [23][24][22]. Our keyword difficulty article goes deeper.

What breaks the rules. Google defines link spam as "the practice of creating links to or from a site primarily for the purpose of manipulating search rankings," and its examples include "Buying or selling links for ranking purposes" [25]. Paid links are allowed when they carry a nofollow or sponsored rel attribute [25]. And from Google's hiring advice: "You should never have to link to an SEO." [1]

Good links for a local business come from suppliers that list their providers, professional associations, local news, community sponsorships, and original data others want to cite. A link gap report, such as Ahrefs' Link Intersect or Semrush's Backlink Gap, lists the sites that link to your competitors but not to you [26][27].

Content that earns links and AI citations

Direct answer

Google gives the same advice for AI answers as for search: make original, specific pages that are useful beyond what already ranks. Google says its generative AI features are rooted in its core ranking and quality systems, so organic SEO is the foundation for both. Lab research on AI answers found that adding sources, quotations and statistics raised a page's visibility.

Google's generative AI guide makes the connection directly: "The best practices for SEO continue to be relevant because our generative AI features on Google Search are rooted in our core Search ranking and quality systems." [3] Its advice for AI features reads like its advice for everything else: "Focus on making unique, non-commodity content that visitors from Search and your own readers will find helpful and satisfying." [28]

The research points the same way. In the peer-reviewed GEO study, researchers rewrote web pages nine ways and measured how visible each version was in AI-generated answers. Adding citations, quotations and statistics produced a 30-40% relative improvement on one visibility metric, while keyword stuffing, tested live on Perplexity, did 10% worse than the unchanged pages [29]. These are lab results from an engine built on Google's top five results and GPT-3.5, not a promise for any site, and the authors did not test effects on rankings [29].

What that means for a business site:

  • Publish facts only you have: prices or ranges, timelines, policies, credentials, your process.
  • Cite your sources, especially for health and cost claims.
  • Answer the questions customers actually ask, in full sentences that make sense on their own.
  • Don't reformat everything into fragments. Google says "There's no requirement to break your content into tiny pieces for AI to better understand it." [3]

Spam policies: what gets sites demoted

Direct answer

Google's spam policies name the tactics that get pages demoted or removed, including link spam, scaled content abuse, expired domain abuse, site reputation abuse and doorway pages. The current version, updated August 28, 2026, also covers attempts to manipulate generative AI responses in Google Search. Google says sites that violate its policies may rank lower or not appear in results at all.

PolicyWhat Google says it covers
Link spamLinks made "primarily for the purpose of manipulating search rankings," including bought links and low-quality directory links
Scaled content abuseWhen "many pages are generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users," whether by automation, people or both
Expired domain abuseBuying an expired domain to host content "that provides little to no value to users"; an old domain used for a genuine new site is fine
Site reputation abuseAnnounced March 5, 2024 and enforced from May 5, 2024; tightened November 19, 2024, when Google said first-party involvement does not change the content's third-party nature
Doorway abusePages "created to rank for specific, similar search queries," including city pages that funnel users to one page
AI manipulation"attempting to manipulate generative AI responses in Google Search"

Every row comes from Google's own pages: the spam policies, the March 2024 announcement and the November 2024 update [25][6][30].

Two of these matter most to businesses that serve several towns or offer many services. Scaled content abuse applies "no matter whether content is produced through automation, human efforts, or some combination of human and automated processes" [6]. Doorway abuse covers near-identical city pages [25]. The fix for both is the same: build a page for a place or a service only when you have something specific to say about it.

The AI item is new territory. Google's generative AI guide warns that building pages for each of the related searches its AI runs, when done "primarily to manipulate rankings or generative AI responses in Google Search," violates its scaled content abuse policy [3]. It adds: "However, seeking inauthentic "mentions" across the web isn't as helpful as it might seem." [3]

Enforcement is not theoretical. Google's dashboard lists confirmed spam updates dated August 26, 2025, and March 24, June 24, August 18 and September 24, 2026 [9].

Key takeaway

If a tactic's main purpose is to manipulate rankings or AI answers rather than help a searcher, Google's spam policies already name it.

What a place on page one is worth

Direct answer

Clicks concentrate at the top of page one. In Advanced Web Ranking's July 2026 US desktop data, the first three organic results took about 92% of the clicks that reached the top ten, and Sistrix measured well under 1% for every position on page two. On searches with an AI Overview, clicks drop further, so track clicks as well as rankings.

Stat

On Google US desktop in July 2026, the first organic result averaged a 20.02% click-through rate, the second 10.36% and the third 3.89%, across more than 1.75 million US results pages a month (Advanced Web Ranking) [31]

The studies measure different things, so read each on its own terms:

StudyData and periodFinding
Advanced Web RankingMore than 1.75 million US results pages a month, July 2026The first three results took about 92% of top-ten clicks [31]
SistrixOver 80 million keywords, partner Search Console data, 2020First result 28.5%; every page-two position well under 1% [15]
BacklinkoSearch Console data via Semrush, data revised 2022First result 27.6%; 0.63% of searchers clicked page two [32]
Pew Research CenterBrowsing by 900 US adults, March 2025Clicked a result on 8% of visits with an AI summary, 15% without one [33]
Ahrefs300,000 keywords, December 2023 against December 2025First-result CTR on AI Overview keywords fell from 7.3% to 1.6% [34]

Click-through rates also swing by industry. In AWR's July 2026 data, the first result's all-device CTR ranged from 12.2% in Law, Government & Politics to 31.2% in Arts & Entertainment, with Health & Fitness at 13.3% near the bottom, which AWR describes as "heavily intercepted by AI answers and knowledge panels" [31].

Two honest conclusions follow. First, the first few results earn most of the clicks; Backlinko went as far as saying "ranking on the first page" may not be a worthy SEO goal [32]. Second, clicks are thin everywhere below them: in AWR's desktop data, results 11 to 20 earned between 0.27% and 0.88% each, about the same as results 7 to 10 [31]. Our first page guarantee promises at least a set share of the agreed phrases on the first page in each of the three places by each milestone, never a particular position or more traffic, and on each milestone date we record, for every phrase, whether your business shows up in the map results, in the organic results and in an AI answer.

AI Overviews change the math on the searches where they appear. AWR found them on 43% of sampled desktop results pages in July 2026, and on those pages the first organic result averaged a 9.69% CTR, against 20.02% across all results pages [31]. Ahrefs measured a 58% lower CTR for the top-ranking page when an AI Overview was present [34]. Google disputes third-party reports of dramatic declines, saying total organic click volume has been "relatively stable" year over year [35]. Seer Interactive found that brands cited inside an AI Overview earned 120% more organic clicks per impression than when they were not cited, though still fewer than on searches with no AI Overview [36]. Our organic click-through rate article has the full numbers.

Measuring organic progress: positions, clicks and share

Direct answer

Measure organic SEO in Google Search Console first: clicks, impressions, click-through rate and average position, by query and by page. Add rank checks made from the market you serve, content and link gap reports against the pages already on page one, and a fixed phrase list checked on set dates. Treat every third-party score as an estimate, not Google's data.

Search Console is Google's own data for your site, and its definitions matter. Click-through rate is "(clicks ÷ impressions)". Position is "the topmost position occupied by a link to your property", averaged across searches, and an AI Overview "occupies a single position in search results" [37]. A result nobody sees gets no impression and no position: if a page sits on page three and the searcher only views page one, its position is not recorded for that search [37].

Rank trackers and SEO suites fill the gaps, with one caution Google spells out: "these tools don't have access to Google's internal ranking data." [1] Use each tool for what it can show:

MeasureSourceWhat it tells you
Clicks, impressions, CTR, positionGoogle Search ConsoleGoogle's data for your site; position averages the results people saw [37]
Positions from your marketA rank tracker set to your cityA snapshot from one place; Google says results depend on location and settings [14]
Keyword difficultyAhrefs KD, Semrush KD%An estimate from competitors' links; each tool calculates it differently [22]
Content gapAhrefs Content Gap, Semrush Keyword GapKeywords competitors rank for and you don't [38][39]
Link gapAhrefs Link Intersect, Semrush Backlink GapSites that link to competitors and not to you
Share of voiceAhrefs, SemrushYour share of estimated clicks, weighted by clicks or volume [40][41]

The share in our first page guarantee is simpler than share of voice. In the map results and the organic results, it is the number of phrases on your list that count there, divided by the number of phrases on your list; in AI answers, it is divided by the number of phrases on your list for which Google shows an AI answer when we check. It is a plain count, with no weighting by clicks or volume.

On timing, Google is deliberately careful. Its starter guide says: "Some changes might take effect in a few hours, others could take several months. In general, you likely want to wait a few weeks to assess whether your work had beneficial effects in Google Search results." [2] Until June 2026, Google's "Do you need an SEO?" page also said results typically take four months to a year; the June 5, 2026 revision removed that sentence [42][1].

Stat

Ahrefs found that only 1.74% of a million random new URLs reached Google's top ten within a year, or 6.11% of the non-empty English pages [43]

Those were random pages with no targeted work behind them, which is why a plan starts with measurement. Of the pages that did reach the top ten, 40.82% got there within a month, and Ahrefs suggests revisiting content still outside the top ten after about six months [43]. Ranking pages also tend to be old: 72.9% of top-ten pages were more than three years old [43].

Google's own hiring advice tells owners to ask any SEO: "What kind of results do you expect to see, and in what timeframe? How do you measure your success?" [1] Those three questions frame any organic program, in-house or hired. Our guides on how long SEO takes to work and SEO competitor analysis cover both in depth.

Where the first page guarantee fits

Direct answer

Our first page guarantee puts the measurement in this guide into writing. We guarantee up to 50% of the key phrases you could rank for will rank on the first page of Google. The share follows a dated schedule and is measured separately in each of the three places. It never promises a position or every phrase, and it is offered after an assessment, only if you qualify.

Google's hiring advice starts from the right place:

We agree, and we don't promise one. Google tells owners to walk away from anyone who promises the top result, and to expect realistic estimates of improvement instead [1]. The first page guarantee answers the three questions Google says to ask: what results, by when, and how they are measured.

  • What: up to 50% of the key phrases a business could rank for, on the first page of Google. At the start we build a list of every variation your customers could type to find a business like yours, and we agree it with you in writing.
  • Where: the three places are your Google Business Profile in Google's local map results, page one of the organic results, and AI suggestions and answers.
  • When: at least 20% of the agreed phrases on the first page within 90 days, 40% within 180 days and 50% within the first year, measured separately in each of the three places at each milestone. Google shows an AI answer for only some searches, so the written terms define how the AI share is measured.
  • What it is not: it does not promise every phrase, any particular position, or traffic, calls, leads or sales.

The method behind it is the measurement described above, applied phrase by phrase. We build the phrase universe from your services, the modifiers people add and the places you serve. For each phrase and each place, we pull every first-page competitor and measure what they have: content depth and topics, on-page elements, referring domains, Google Business Profile categories, review count, rating and pace, photos, citations and presence in AI answers. Then we match every signal and exceed it, and we set the share we can promise from the measured gap, with margin built into the schedule. That is our method, not a law of Google's, and Google still decides every ranking. It applies Ahrefs' rule of thumb on links [22] to every signal we can measure.

Frequently asked questions

What is organic SEO?

Organic SEO is the work of earning unpaid places in Google's regular results. It covers on-page work (matching intent and covering topics), technical work (making pages crawlable, indexable and fast) and off-page work (earning links and mentions). Google ranks those results with automated systems that weigh hundreds of factors, and it says no one can pay for a higher organic place [4][1].

Is organic SEO free?

Appearing in organic results is free. Google says it "never accepts money to include or rank sites" in its search results and that it costs nothing to appear there [1]. The work itself takes time or budget: pages, fixes, links and measurement. Google also notes that if you run a small local business, you can probably do much of the work yourself [1].

How long does organic SEO take?

Google's current guidance says some changes take effect in a few hours and others take several months, and suggests waiting a few weeks before judging your work [2]. In Ahrefs' sample, only 1.74% of random new pages reached the top ten within a year, while 40.82% of the pages that did reach it got there within a month [43]. Those were pages with no targeted work behind them.

What is the difference between organic and paid search?

Paid results are ads that a business pays Google to show. Organic results are the unpaid listings that Google's ranking systems order by relevance and usefulness, and Google says it never accepts money to include or rank sites in them [1]. Ads stop when the budget stops. Organic places depend on the pages, links and signals behind them, which take longer to build.

Do backlinks still matter?

Yes. Google says it uses links to judge relevance and to find new pages, and PageRank remains part of its core ranking systems, though Google calls it one signal among many [16][5]. Links bought to manipulate rankings break Google's spam policies [25]. For AI Overviews, Ahrefs found brand mentions correlated more strongly with visibility than backlinks did, a correlation rather than proof of cause [44].

Does AI change organic SEO?

It changes the results page more than the work. Google says its AI features are rooted in its core ranking and quality systems, and that no special files or markup are needed to appear in them [3][21]. What changes is clicks: on searches with an AI Overview, Pew and Ahrefs both measured fewer clicks on regular results [33][34]. Our AI search optimization guide covers it in full.
