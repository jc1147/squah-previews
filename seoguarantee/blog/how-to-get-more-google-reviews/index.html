How to Get More Google Reviews (Without Breaking the Rules)

Ask every customer, make it one tap, reply to every review, and stay inside Google's policy and the FTC's rule: no incentives, no gating, no scripts. Templates for text, email and in person.

Ask every customer, soon after the job, with a direct link to your Google review form, and reply to every review you get. What you never do matters as much: Google bans review incentives of any kind, asking only happy customers and scripting what people write, and the FTC's 2024 rule makes fake reviews unlawful.

The short answer

Direct answer

Google's own review tips suggest asking customers for reviews, for example by sending them a Google link or showing a QR code [1], and its policy allows asking for honest reviews without incentives [2]. So: get your review link, ask every customer when the job is done, make leaving a review one tap, and reply to every review. Never pay, never filter who you ask, never script.

Reviews pay off twice. Google says more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking [3], and customers read them: in BrightLocal's 2026 survey of 1,002 US adults, 97% read reviews for local businesses and 47% won't use a business with fewer than 20 reviews [4].

Stat

74% of consumers look for reviews written in the last three months (BrightLocal, 2026) [4].

That is why a steady flow beats a one-time push. A burst of reviews last spring does less for the customer reading today than a few from last week.

Get your review link

Direct answer

Google's review tips say you can ask customers to visit a Google link or scan a QR code, and link to Google's own instructions for creating both [1]. Create the link once, test it on a phone, and use the same link everywhere you ask: text messages, emails, receipts and a QR code card at the front desk. One tap should open the review form.

The printed QR code does a second job. A customer can take the card and leave the review later on their own phone, which keeps you clear of Google's ban on pressuring people to leave a review while they are on your premises [2].

When and how to ask

Direct answer

Ask every customer, not just the ones you expect to be happy, soon after the service while it is fresh, through the channel they already use with you. Keep the ask short and neutral: you would value an honest review. Google bans selectively soliciting positive reviews, pressuring customers to review while on your premises, and requests for specific content [2].

Staff can do the asking, within limits. Internet Archive captures show Google added two clauses to its policy between February 15 and February 23, 2026 [5]: businesses may not ask staff to collect a set number of reviews, and may not ask for reviews that include specific content, including content that names a staff member [2]. Staff can hand every customer the card or send the link. They can't have a quota to hit or a script that asks customers to mention them.

Ask everyone the same way. Asking only the customers you expect to be happy, known as review gating, is banned by Google [2], and FTC staff say that although the FTC's rule has no specific ban on it, the practice could violate the FTC Act [6].

Healthcare practices have extra limits. The ADA's guidance says reviews should not be posted by anyone employed by the practice, strongly recommends against paying for reviews, and notes that some jurisdictions may prohibit soliciting online reviews by patients [7]. The ethics codes for psychologists and social workers bar soliciting testimonials from current clients [8][9]; whether a review request counts is a question for your board or counsel. Read our article on HIPAA marketing rules before you ask patients anything.

Templates: text, email and in person

Direct answer

Keep every request short, neutral and identical for every customer: thank them, ask for an honest review, give the link, and stop. Don't mention stars, don't suggest what to write, don't name staff and don't offer anything in return. The templates below follow Google's policy as written: no incentives, no selective asking and no requested content [2].

Text message. "Hi [first name], thanks for choosing [business] today. If you have a minute, we'd appreciate an honest review on Google: [link]"

Review request email. Subject: "How was your visit to [business]?" Body: "Hi [first name], thank you for choosing us. If you're willing, we'd appreciate an honest review on Google. It takes a minute: [link]. Thank you, [your name]"

In person. "Thanks for coming in. If you'd like to share how it went, this card has a link to our Google reviews. Whenever suits you." Hand over the card and let them go; don't wait while they write.

For healthcare, keep every detail of the care out of the message. "Thanks for visiting [practice] today" is enough.

Replying to every review

Direct answer

Reply to every review, positive and negative, in a line or two that sounds like a person. Google's tips say to be conversational, not promotional [1]. In BrightLocal's 2026 survey, 89% of consumers said they expect owners to respond, and generic or templated replies put off 50% [4]. Healthcare practices have extra limits on what a reply may say [10][11].

Google checks replies before they post, which usually takes up to 10 minutes and sometimes up to 30 days, and a customer can still change their review after reading your reply [12]. A calm reply to a complaint is your one public chance to have that review revised.

On negative reviews, Google's tips note that a mix of positive and negative feedback often feels more trustworthy [1]. Thank the reviewer, take the details offline, and never threaten anyone: the FTC's rule bans suppressing reviews through unfounded legal threats, physical threats or intimidation [13].

For healthcare, OCR has taken action over replies that disclosed patient information. New Vision Dental paid $23,000, with OCR's director calling such disclosures "a clear NO" [10], and a psychiatric practice, Manasa Health Center, paid $30,000 over its reply to a negative review [11]. The ADA's model reply stays in broad "all patient" terms and never references a specific person or incident [14]. More on what reviews do for rankings in do Google reviews help SEO.

What never to do: incentives, gating, fake reviews

Direct answer

Never pay for reviews or offer discounts, free services or gifts for them; never ask only happy customers; never post reviews yourself or through staff; never buy them. Google bans paid reviews, incentives and gating [2]. The FTC's rule, in force since October 21, 2024, bans fake reviews and incentives conditioned on a positive or negative review [6][13].

The FTC announced the rule on August 14, 2024, and says it lets the agency seek civil penalties against knowing violators [13]. The rule makes it an unfair or deceptive practice for a business to write, create or sell a review that misrepresents whether the reviewer exists or used the business, or to buy a review it knew or should have known misrepresents those things [15]. It also covers insider reviews: reviews by a company's officers or managers need a clear and conspicuous disclosure of the relationship [15].

Google is stricter than the FTC. FTC staff say the rule itself does not ban an incentive with no sentiment condition [6]; Google bans every review incentive, from payment to discounts to free goods or services [2].

Stat

Google blocked or removed more than 292 million policy-violating reviews in 2025 [16].

Google also says a spike in reviews can lead it to pause new reviews on the profile, alert the owner and display a notification banner [16]. Whitespark's experts rank reports of review gating sixth and fake review reports seventh among suspension risks for a Business Profile [17]. And the offers keep coming: 11% of consumers in BrightLocal's 2026 survey said they had been offered an incentive to write a positive review [4]. Any vendor who offers to "boost" your reviews belongs on your list of SEO red flags.

Checklist: getting Google reviews the right way

Frequently asked questions

How do I get a link for customers to leave a Google review?

Google's review tips say you can ask customers to visit a Google link or scan a QR code, and link to Google's own instructions for creating both [1]. Create the link, test it on your phone, and use the same link in texts, emails, receipts and a printed QR code card. A customer who taps it should land straight on your review form.

Can I offer a discount for a Google review?

No. Google's policy lists payment, discounts, and free goods or services as banned incentives for posting a review [2]. The FTC's rule separately bans incentives conditioned on a positive or negative review [13]. FTC staff say the rule doesn't reach incentives with no sentiment condition [6], but Google's ban covers every review posted on Google, so a discount for a review breaks Google's policy either way.

Is it illegal to buy reviews?

The FTC's rule, in force since October 21, 2024, makes it an unfair or deceptive practice for a business to buy a review it knew or should have known misrepresents the reviewer or their experience [15], and bans incentives conditioned on a review's sentiment [13]. The FTC says the rule lets it seek civil penalties against knowing violators [13]. Google bans paid reviews outright [2]. For your own situation, ask your counsel.

Can I ask only happy customers for reviews?

Not on Google. Its policy bans discouraging negative reviews and selectively soliciting positive ones [2]. FTC staff say the rule has no specific ban on the practice but that it could violate the FTC Act [6], and Whitespark's experts rank reports of review gating sixth among suspension risks for a Business Profile [17]. Ask every customer the same way.

How many reviews should I ask for each month?

Ask every customer; Google sets no target, and it bans giving staff review quotas [2]. What matters is a steady flow: Sterling Sky's correlational study found the reviews a business gained this month mattered more than its total [18], and Google can pause reviews on a profile when they spike [16]. Benchmark against how many reviews the businesses in your map results gained in the last 90 days.
