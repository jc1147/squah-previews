Google Business Profile for Dentists: A Practice Setup Guide

How to set up a Google Business Profile for a dental practice under Google's own rules: practitioner profiles, the right categories and services, photos and hours, reviews and replies that protect patient privacy, and tracking.

A dental practice's Google Business Profile is what puts it in the map results, so set it up the way Google's rules describe: one accurate primary category, every treatment listed as a service, exact hours and real photos. Then earn reviews steadily and reply to them without ever confirming that a reviewer is a patient.

The short answer

Direct answer

Set up the profile, choose the one primary category that says what the practice is (for most general practices, Dentist), add every treatment as a service, keep hours exact and photos real, and reply to reviews in general terms. Google says the categories you select affect your local ranking, and that complete, accurate profiles are more likely to show up [1][2].

Stat

In Whitespark's 2026 survey of 47 local search experts, Business Profile signals carried 32% of the weight in map-pack rankings, and the primary category was the highest-scoring single factor [3].

The survey measures expert opinion, not Google's code [3]. Google's own summary is that local results are "mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity", and that there is "no way to request or pay for a better local ranking" [2]. The profile is where you work on the first and third of those. For the wider picture, see our dental local SEO guide.

Practice and practitioner profiles

Direct answer

Google treats dentists as individual practitioners, and a practitioner's profile may carry a title such as Dr. For a solo dentist at a branded practice, Google's guideline is one shared profile named "[brand]: [dentist's name]". Whatever you set up, keep one profile per business [4].

The ground rules come from Google's Business Profile help pages:

  • Only businesses that meet customers face to face are eligible [33], and a virtual office or a P.O. box doesn't qualify [4].
  • There should be only one profile per business [4].
  • Adding unnecessary information to the name isn't permitted and could get the profile suspended [4].

Whitespark's experts rank a second profile in the same category at the same address third among negative factors [3].

Verification is Google's call: it picks the method, and where video verification is offered, it recommends that option [5]. If a profile is suspended, appeal through Google's tool and don't create a new profile while the appeal is open; appeal decisions can take up to 5 business days [6].

Categories and services for dental offices

Direct answer

Choose one primary category that completes the sentence "This business IS a"; for most general practices that is Dentist. Add only the few extra categories that are also true, never use categories as keywords, and list every treatment as a service with a description. Google publishes no public category list; PlePer's 2026 US list confirms the dental options shown below [4][7].

The rules around them:

  • One primary category, plus up to 9 more; the category in the first field is the primary [8].
  • You can't create your own category [1], and Google adds broader parent categories itself and reads category signals from your website and mentions across the web [4].
  • Adding or editing a category can trigger a new verification [1].
  • An incorrect primary category was the second-worst negative factor in Whitespark's survey [3].

A specialist category belongs to a practice that is that specialty. A general practice that places implants is a dentist that offers implants, and the ADA Code asks general dentists to avoid communications that "express or imply specialization" [9]. The Services list is where implants, aligners and whitening belong. Google lets businesses add custom services with a price and a description, suggests services by business type, and says a listed service may be highlighted when someone searches for it [10]. Photos, hours and attributes

Direct answer

Upload real photos of the building, the team and the rooms patients see; Google says an image should represent reality, without significant alterations or heavy filters or AI. List your regular hours exactly and set special hours for holidays. Be wary of advice about new-patient or insurance attributes: we found no Google documentation of either for dental profiles, so put those details on your website [11][4].

On photos, Google's line is that "the image should represent reality" [11]. Leave patient faces out unless your counsel has approved it: HIPAA's Privacy Rule lists full-face photographs among its identifiers [12]. Whitespark's experts rate geotagged photos a myth [3], so skip that step.

On hours, being open at the time of the search ranked fifth among Whitespark's 2026 map-pack factors [3]. Sterling Sky first reported the effect in December 2023, and Google has not confirmed it [13]. The answer is not to stretch your hours: Google asks for your regular customer-facing hours [4], and special hours cover closures of up to 6 days in a row [14].

On the description, Google allows no links of any type [4]. Posts come in three types, Updates, Offers and Events, and posts older than six months are archived unless a date range is set [15]; Whitespark's experts rate the number of posts a myth [3], so post when you have something patients need to know.

Reviews and HIPAA-safe replies

Direct answer

Ask every patient for a review with your Google review link or QR code, and never offer anything in return, filter who you ask or suggest what to write: Google bans all three. Reply to every review in general terms that never confirm the person is a patient; HHS has penalized dental practices over replies that disclosed patient information [16][17][18][23].

Google says "More reviews and positive ratings can help your business's local ranking." [2] The rules for getting them:

  • Google bans incentives such as payment, discounts, or free goods or services for any review, asking only happy customers, pressure to review while patients are in the office, and requests for specific content, including staff names [17].
  • The FTC's rule, in force since October 21, 2024, bans businesses from fake reviews, incentives tied to a particular sentiment and suppressing reviews with threats [19][20].
  • The ADA says reviews should not be posted by anyone employed by the practice and recommends strongly against paying for them [21].

Replies matter to patients. In Tebra's research, 71% of patients said they trust providers more when they respond to negative reviews, yet 45% of providers said they never do [22]. The danger is in what a reply says. HHS's Office for Civil Rights imposed a $50,000 civil money penalty on a North Carolina dental practice that disclosed a patient's information in a reply to a negative review [18]. New Vision Dental paid $23,000, and OCR's director said of such disclosures: "This is a clear NO." [23]

The ADA's guidance is to respond in very broad "all patient" terms and "Never reference a specific person or incident." [24] A reply of our own making in that style: "Thank you for taking the time to share this; we want every patient to have a good experience, and we'd welcome a call from anyone who wants to talk about theirs." Google checks replies before they post, usually within 10 minutes but sometimes up to 30 days, and reviewers can edit their review after reading your reply [25]. Our article on HIPAA marketing rules covers the privacy side in full.

Tracking bookings and inquiries

Direct answer

Use the profile's Performance report to see the searches that found you and what people did next, and tag your website link so visits from the profile show up in your analytics. Keep one main phone number on the profile and the website, and keep ad pixels off booking and new-patient pages unless counsel has cleared them, because HHS says tracking on such pages can reach patient information [26][27].

The Performance report lists the search terms people used to find your business, updated at the start of each month, and includes activity from both unpaid results and Google Ads [26]. Google removed the profile's chat and call history on July 31, 2024 [28]; in select regions, verified profiles can add a WhatsApp or text messaging option instead [29].

Phone numbers need care. Whitespark's experts count mismatched details or tracking phone numbers across the web against a business [3], so keep the practice's main number consistent everywhere. On the website, HHS's tracking bulletin says that when a scheduling page gives a tracking vendor access to protected health information, the vendor is a business associate and a BAA is required, and that a cookie banner is not a valid HIPAA authorization [27].

The profile also feeds Google's AI features. Google lists an up-to-date Business Profile among the basics for AI Overviews and AI Mode [30], and Ask Maps, launched in the US on March 12, 2026, draws on places and reviews [31]. Frequently asked questions

Should each dentist have their own Google Business Profile?

Google's guidelines treat dentists as individual practitioners and allow practitioner profiles that include a title such as Dr. [4]. For a solo dentist who is the public face of a branded practice, the guideline is one shared profile named "[brand]: [dentist's name]" [4]. Whatever the setup, keep to one profile per business and avoid duplicates at the same address, which Whitespark's experts rate a strong negative [3].

Which category should a dental office use?

The one that completes "This business IS a": for most general practices, Dentist. Google says to use the fewest categories that describe your core business and never to use categories as keywords [4]. PlePer's 2026 US list also confirms Dental clinic, Cosmetic dentist, Emergency dental service and the specialist categories [7]. A specialist category belongs to a practice that is that specialty.

Can a dentist reply to reviews on Google?

Google lets owners reply, and checks each reply before it posts [25]. Keep every reply general: never confirm that the reviewer is a patient or mention their care. HHS has penalized dental practices over replies that disclosed patient information [23], including a $50,000 civil money penalty [18], and the ADA's model reply speaks to "all patients" without naming anyone [24].

How do dental offices get more Google reviews?

Ask every patient at the same point in the visit, and hand over your Google review link or a QR code, which Google allows [16]. Google bans incentives, asking only happy patients and scripted content [17], and the ADA says staff should not post reviews [21]. Aim for a steady flow: in BrightLocal's 2026 survey, 74% of consumers looked for reviews written in the last three months [32].

Do dental offices need photos on their profile?

Yes, and real ones. Google's photo guidance asks for images without significant alterations, heavy filters or AI [11]. Show the building, the entrance, the team and the treatment rooms. Leave patient faces out unless counsel has approved it, since HIPAA's Privacy Rule lists full-face photographs among its identifiers [12]. Geotagging photos won't help: Whitespark's experts rate it a myth [3].
