How therapists and counselors get clients from Google: a Business Profile where eligible, specialty pages, directory listings, the ethics codes on testimonials, privacy-safe tracking and ads, and what AI answers show.

Therapists get clients from Google by showing up in three places: the map results through a Google Business Profile, the organic results through clear specialty pages, and the directories people browse when they choose a therapist. The rules are stricter than in most fields: professional ethics codes limit testimonials, and HIPAA guidance and FTC enforcement shape how practices track visitors and advertise.

This guide covers how people find a therapist, how to measure the practices already on page one, and the steps that grow a caseload without crossing those lines. Where a rule applies, we say what it says, who it binds and when it took effect, and leave how it applies to your practice to your counsel, your licensing board or your association's ethics office.

How people search for a therapist today

Direct answer

No independent study of how people search for a therapist has been published that we could find, so measure your own market. In our Las Vegas check, "therapist" showed three counseling practices in the map results, organic results made up mostly of directories such as Psychology Today, and no AI answer [1]. A practice needs both a profile and directory listings.

We searched 12 commercial phrases from Las Vegas on September 27, 2026, on desktop, and recorded what page one held [1]. For "therapist", the map results held three local counseling practices [1]. Most of the organic results were directories and national platforms, including Psychology Today, Yelp, GoodTherapy, Mental Health Match and Talkspace [1]. There was no AI Overview, and across all 12 searches Google showed one for only two [1]. One capture is a snapshot, not a rule, but the pattern is clear enough to plan around: the map is where an individual practice competes directly, and directories hold much of the rest.

The only search numbers we found for this field come from a directory selling listings. Psychology Today says its directory shows up first on Google for therapy-seekers 96.2% of the time and claims 971,000 unique weekly US users [2]. It publishes no method for either figure, so treat both as vendor claims.

Reviews shape the choice as well. In Tebra's 2025 survey of patients who had seen a healthcare provider, therapists included, 51% said reviews matter more when choosing a specialist than a primary care provider [3].

Capacity is the other half of the picture:

Stat

In the American Psychological Association's 2025 practitioner survey, 46% of psychologists reported no openings for new patients, 40% had a waitlist, and 38% did not accept any form of health insurance (1,742 respondents; a 6.6% completion rate) [4].

Many practices are full. For a new practice, or one with openings, being findable is what fills the calendar.

Key takeaway

Measure every phrase in each place separately; the map, the organic results and an AI answer are won with different signals, and directories sit in the middle of all three.

Measure the practices already on page one

Direct answer

Before you spend on marketing, measure the practices that already hold page one for your specialties. For the map results, record their categories, reviews and distance. For the organic results, record which directories and practice pages rank, and what those pages cover. For AI answers, record which pages Google cites. Your plan is the gap.

Google's own advice on hiring an SEO tells business owners to ask what results to expect, in what timeframe, and how success will be measured [5]. It is also blunt about promises:

We agree with it. Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking [6], and that third-party SEO tools don't have access to its internal ranking data [5]. What you can measure is the gap:

The phrase list: specialties x modalities x places

Direct answer

Build the phrase list from three columns: the concerns and specialties you treat (anxiety, depression, trauma, couples), the modalities and formats people search for (EMDR, CBT, online therapy, in-person), and the places you serve. Check it against autocomplete, Keyword Planner, your profile's search terms and Search Console, and keep what clients actually type.

Concerns and specialtiesModalities, formats and modifiersPlaces
anxiety, depression, trauma, grief, couples, family, teens, addictionEMDR, CBT, online therapy, in-person, therapist, counselor, psychologist, accepts insurance, near meyour city, its neighborhoods, the towns clients drive from

Write the list in clients' words, and include only what you are licensed and trained to offer. Then check it against four sources of real searches:

  1. Autocomplete. Google says its predictions reflect real searches and vary with the location a query comes from [9].
  2. Keyword Planner. Its volumes are averages over close variants, rounded, and tied to the location you choose [10].
  3. Your Business Profile. The Performance report lists the search terms people used to find your profile, updated at the start of each month [11].
  4. Search Console. Its queries dimension groups your site's data by the searches people typed [12].

Keep the small phrases. In Ahrefs' US database, almost 93% of keywords get fewer than 10 searches a month [13]. A specialty plus your neighborhood may show no volume and still be the exact search of someone ready to book. Our guide to local SEO keyword research covers list-building in depth.

The Business Profile for a private practice

Direct answer

A Google Business Profile is how a practice appears in the map results, but not every practice qualifies: Google's guidelines limit profiles to businesses that make face-to-face contact with customers, and a virtual office is not eligible [14][15]. If you see clients in person, choose the most specific category, use your real name, keep hours accurate and list your services.

Eligibility. Google says only businesses that make "face-to-face contact with customers" are eligible for a Business Profile [14], and that a location that is a virtual office isn't eligible [15]. A practice that sees clients only online should read those rules before creating a profile; its own website and directory listings remain open to it either way.

Categories. Google publishes no public category list, so marketers rely on compiled ones. PlePer's US list includes Psychologist, Psychotherapist, Psychiatrist, Counselor, Mental health clinic, Mental health service, Marriage or relationship counselor, Family counselor, EMDR psychotherapist and Addiction treatment center [16]. A profile has one primary category and up to nine more, and the first field is the primary [17]. Google's guidelines say to choose the fewest categories that describe your core business, to pick ones that complete the statement "This business IS a", and never to use categories as keywords [15]. Google says the categories you choose affect your local ranking [18].

Name and practitioners. Use your real-world name; Google's guidelines say adding unnecessary information to the name can get a profile suspended [15]. The guidelines allow profiles for individual practitioners, which may include a title or degree certification [15]. Before adding practitioner profiles at a shared address, read those rules closely: Whitespark's experts rank a same-category profile at the same address among the strongest negative factors [7].

Hours. Google's guidelines ask for your regular customer-facing hours and say businesses that operate only by appointment shouldn't provide hours [15]. Whitespark's experts rank "business is open at time of search" fifth among local pack factors, and the same report says "do NOT list your hours other than what they actually are" [7]. List only hours you truly keep.

Services and privacy. List each service with a plain description; Google says a listed service may be highlighted when people search for it [19]. Photos should show the office and the people who work there, never clients. Check the Performance report's search terms every month [11]. Specialty and service pages that build trust

Direct answer

Give each specialty and service its own page: who it helps, how you work, what a first session looks like, fees and insurance, and your license and training. In Whitespark's 2026 survey, a dedicated page for each service was the top factor for local organic rankings and the second for AI search visibility [7]. Clear, specific pages build trust with readers and with Google.

Mental health content is exactly where Google raises the bar. Google says: "our systems give even more weight to content that aligns with strong E-E-A-T for topics that could significantly impact the health, financial stability, or safety of people" [20]. E-E-A-T stands for experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust; Google says trust matters most and encourages accurate bylines where readers expect them [20]. Put the clinician's name, license type and training on every page they write.

Case examples need care. The APA Ethics Code says psychologists do not disclose identifiable client information in public media unless they take reasonable steps to disguise the person, the person has consented in writing, or there is legal authorization [21]. It is the psychologists' code; check your own profession's code before writing about any client, even in disguise.

Fees belong on the page. In APA's 2025 survey, 38% of psychologists did not accept any form of health insurance [4]. Whatever your model, a clear page on fees, insurance and reduced-fee options answers the question before the first call.

Build depth, not duplicates. Google's spam policies treat pages created to rank for similar searches that funnel visitors to one page as doorway abuse, and pages generated at scale to manipulate rankings as scaled content abuse [22]. One strong page per specialty beats a thin copy for every nearby town.

Checklist: a specialty page

Directories and citations (Psychology Today and others)

Direct answer

Directories carry real weight in mental health: in our Las Vegas check they held most of the organic results for "therapist" [1], and in Yext's analysis, listings made up 52.6% of healthcare citations in AI answers [23]. A complete, consistent directory profile is part of how clients and AI tools find you, alongside your own profile and site.

Directories rank for the directory; your profile and website rank for you. Use both. Psychology Today lists its directory at $29.95 a month on its sign-up page [2]. Compare what each directory costs with the inquiries it produces, and keep every profile complete and identical to your website and Business Profile.

Listings now feed AI answers too. Yext, which sells listings management, analyzed 6.8 million citations in AI answers and found listings made up 52.6% of the citations in healthcare [23]. Whitespark's experts give citations 6% of the weight for map rankings but 13% for AI search visibility [7]. Microsoft tells local businesses to register with Bing Places so that address, hours and contact details stay eligible for AI answers [24].

Consistency is the rule. Whitespark's experts count the absence of a crawlable name, address and phone on your website as a negative factor, and a mismatch across the wider data ecosystem as another [7]. Our article on local citations covers the details, and our local SEO guide covers the rest of the map.

Reviews and testimonials: the ethics rules for therapists

Direct answer

The ethics codes for psychologists, counselors and social workers all limit soliciting testimonials from clients. APA Standard 5.05 bars psychologists from soliciting testimonials from current therapy clients, and the ACA and NASW codes set similar limits [21][25][26]. Whether asking for an online review counts is a question for your licensing board or ethics office, so ask before any review program.

What each code says:

CodeWhat it saysWhose code
APA Ethics Code, Standard 5.05Psychologists "do not solicit testimonials from current therapy clients/patients" or from others vulnerable to undue influence [21]American Psychological Association (psychologists)
2014 ACA Code of Ethics, C.3.bCounselors who use testimonials "do not solicit them from current clients, former clients" or others who may be vulnerable to undue influence [25]American Counseling Association (counselors)
NASW Code of Ethics, 4.07(b)Social workers "should not engage in solicitation of testimonial endorsements" from current clients, including asking for consent to reuse a client's earlier statement [26]National Association of Social Workers (social workers)

Check the version before you rely on a number. The APA code took effect June 1, 2003, with amendments effective June 1, 2010 and January 1, 2017, and APA has a revision in progress [21][27]. The ACA's ethics page called the 2014 code the current version until a revision is adopted, with adoption targeted for September 2026 [28]. Standard numbers may change.

Google's rules apply on top of the codes. Google bans incentives such as payment, discounts, or free goods or services for any review, bans asking only happy customers, and bars on-site pressure and requests for specific content [29]. The FTC's rule on consumer reviews, in force since October 21, 2024, bans fake reviews, incentives conditioned on a positive or negative review, and suppressing reviews through unfounded legal threats or intimidation [30][31].

Replies are where therapists get hurt. HHS's Office for Civil Rights reached a resolution with Manasa Health Center, a New Jersey psychiatric practice, which paid $30,000 after it disclosed a patient's protected health information in a reply to a negative online review [32]. OCR's director said after a similar dental case: "This is a clear NO" [33]. The American Dental Association's model reply, written for dentists, shows the safe shape: broad "all patient" terms, and never a reference to a specific person or incident [34]. For a therapist, even acknowledging that someone was a client can disclose too much.

Visibility does not depend on reviews alone. A complete profile, strong specialty pages and consistent directory listings all work without asking anyone for anything.

Tracking, ads and privacy

Direct answer

Mental health marketing carries heavy privacy exposure. HHS guidance says tracking on booking pages may reach protected health information, and that cookie banners are not HIPAA authorizations [35]. The FTC moved to bar BetterHelp and Cerebral from using consumers' health data for most advertising [36][37]. Google treats counseling ads as a sensitive category [38]. Review your tracking with counsel.

The HHS tracking bulletin. On June 20, 2024, a federal court in American Hospital Association v. Becerra vacated one position in HHS's guidance on online tracking: that HIPAA obligations are triggered when a technology connects an IP address with a visit to an unauthenticated public page about specific health conditions or providers [39]. HHS did not appeal [40]. These points remain in the bulletin [35]:

  • Tracking on pages where users log in generally has access to protected health information.
  • Pages where people schedule appointments without logging in may have access to it in certain circumstances; then the tracking vendor is a business associate and "a BAA is required".
  • Cookie banners "do not constitute a valid HIPAA authorization".
  • Disclosing protected health information to tracking vendors for marketing without HIPAA-compliant authorizations would be impermissible.

The bulletin also names "frequency of visits to a therapist" as an example of sensitive information [35]. It is addressed to HIPAA covered entities and business associates; whether it reaches your practice is a question for your counsel.

The FTC cases. In March 2023, the FTC announced a proposed order against BetterHelp ($7.8 million) over sharing "email addresses, IP addresses, and health questionnaire information" with Facebook, Snapchat, Criteo and Pinterest [36]. In April 2024, it announced a proposed order against Cerebral, which it said gave sensitive information of nearly 3.2 million consumers to third parties such as LinkedIn, Snapchat and TikTok through tracking tools; the order would permanently ban Cerebral from using or disclosing consumers' personal and health information to third parties for most marketing or advertising purposes [37]. Separately, in July 2023, the FTC and HHS wrote to about 130 hospital systems and telehealth providers about tracking technologies such as the Meta/Facebook pixel and Google Analytics [41].

Substance use disorder records. Practices that treat substance use disorders may also fall under 42 CFR Part 2. Its 2024 final rule took effect April 16, 2024, with compliance required by February 16, 2026 [42]. HHS says the rule permits use and disclosure of Part 2 records based on a single patient consent given once for all future treatment, payment and health care operations [43]. The rule's definition of a "Program" covers a person, other than a general medical facility, that holds itself out as providing, and provides, substance use disorder diagnosis, treatment or referral for treatment [44]. Other sections set further tests, so whether it covers your practice is a question for your counsel.

Google Ads. Google's health policy lists counseling services for mental health issues like depression, anxiety and addiction among its sensitive categories, so advertiser-curated audiences such as Customer Match can't be used [38]. For US ads for recovery-oriented drug and alcohol addiction services, Google requires certification through the LegitScript program [45].

Stat

In LocaliQ's medians for October 2024 to September 2025, mental health search ads cost $4.22 per click with a 1.85% conversion rate and $141.17 per lead, the highest cost per lead of the healthcare fields it tracked; addiction recovery leads cost $120.30 [46].

LocaliQ reports mental health cost per click rose 42% and cost per lead 146% year on year, from its own client campaigns [46]. Google says it costs nothing to appear in its organic results [5], but that only helps if the site it sends people to is built for privacy.

AI answers and mental health searches

Direct answer

Google says its AI answers need no special optimization and can include local businesses, helped by an accurate Business Profile [47][8]. They appear far more often for questions than for short searches: "therapist" showed none in our Las Vegas check [1]. Clear specialty pages and complete, consistent listings are what AI tools have to work with.

Google's guidance is plain. Its AI features page says: "There are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, nor other special optimizations necessary" [47]. Its generative AI guide says: "Where appropriate, generative AI responses can include product listings, product information, and information about local businesses" [8].

Pew's analysis of browsing data from 900 US adults in March 2025 found an AI summary on 18% of Google searches, on 8% of one- or two-word searches, and on 60% of searches that began with a question word [48]. People may research a concern as a question before they search for a therapist; answer those questions on your specialty pages, in plain language, with sources.

Other assistants localize too. OpenAI says ChatGPT may use an approximate location based on your IP address to provide local results, and draws on third-party providers for local information such as nearby business listings [49]. BrightLocal's 2026 survey found 45% of consumers had used AI tools for local business recommendations, up from 6% a year earlier, and 88% of AI users said they fact-check the reviews an AI tool cites [50].

Accuracy matters more here than anywhere. Google says its AI Overviews "can and will make mistakes" [51]. Search your specialties from your own area each month and correct your own pages where an AI answer describes you wrongly. Skip the shortcuts: Google says you don't need AI text files or special markup, and that Google Search ignores llms.txt files [47][8].

Frequently asked questions

How do new therapists get clients?

Start with what compounds: a Business Profile if your practice meets Google's face-to-face rule, a clear page for each specialty, and complete listings on the directories that rank in your area. Add referral relationships with local physicians and other clinicians. Demand is there: in APA's 2025 survey, 46% of psychologists had no openings for new patients [4]. Being findable is what connects that demand to your calendar.

Do therapists need a Google Business Profile?

A profile is how a practice appears in Google's map results, and in our Las Vegas check the map results for "therapist" showed three local counseling practices [1]. Google's guidelines limit profiles to businesses that make face-to-face contact with customers, and a virtual office is not eligible [14][15]. An online-only practice should read those rules first; its website and directories remain available either way.

Can therapists ask clients for reviews?

The ethics codes speak to testimonials. APA Standard 5.05 bars psychologists from soliciting testimonials from current therapy clients [21]. The ACA code bars counselors from soliciting them from current or former clients, and the NASW code says social workers should not solicit them from current clients [25][26]. Whether an online review request counts is a question for your licensing board or ethics office, so ask before you start.

Is a directory listing enough to get clients?

A directory listing helps the directory rank; your own profile and website help you rank. In our Las Vegas check, directories held most of the organic results for "therapist", while three local practices held the map results [1]. Listings also feed AI answers: in Yext's analysis, listings made up 52.6% of healthcare citations [23]. Use directories alongside your own profile and site, not instead of them.

How long does it take a therapy practice to reach page one?

It depends on the gap between you and the practices already there. Google's starter guide says some changes take effect in a few hours and others take several months, so wait a few weeks before judging [52]. In Ahrefs' 2025 study, 40.82% of pages that reached the top 10 did so within a month, yet only 1.74% of a random sample of new pages got there within a year [53].

Do AI assistants recommend therapists?

They can. Google says its AI answers can include information about local businesses [8]. OpenAI says ChatGPT may use an approximate location and third-party business listings for local results [49]. BrightLocal found 45% of consumers had used AI tools for local recommendations [50]. Short searches such as "therapist" rarely trigger Google's AI answers [48], so complete listings and clear specialty pages matter most.
