Keyword Difficulty: What the Score Measures and What It Misses

Keyword difficulty is a tool's 0 to 100 estimate built mostly from the links of the pages already ranking. How Ahrefs and Semrush calculate it, what it misses, and how to use it to order the work.

Keyword difficulty is an SEO tool's 0 to 100 estimate of how hard it would be to reach the first page of Google's organic results for a keyword, built mostly from the links pointing at the pages already there [1]. It is useful for ordering work and no good as a promise: it ignores your own site, the map results and AI answers, and no tool sees Google's internal ranking data [17].

The short answer

Direct answer

Keyword difficulty (KD) is a score from 0 to 100 that estimates how hard it would be to rank on Google's first page for a keyword. Ahrefs builds it from how many websites link to the current top ten pages [1]; Semrush blends links, authority scores and features of the results page [3]. It is an outside estimate, not Google's measure, and each tool calculates it differently.

Two tools can give the same keyword different scores, and neither is wrong: each calculates difficulty its own way [1]. What matters is using one tool consistently, then checking the actual page-one results yourself.

How Ahrefs calculates KD

Direct answer

Ahrefs pulls the top ten organic pages for a keyword and counts how many websites link to each [1]. The more linking domains those pages have, the higher the KD. Ahrefs calls the score absolute, not relative: it describes how hard the keyword is for an average website, and it doesn't account for your own site's authority or any on-page factors [1][2].

In Ahrefs' words, KD "estimates how hard it would be to rank on the first page of Google" on a 0 to 100 scale [1]. Its help center adds that the score uses the top ten organic results only and "does not take into consideration any 'on-page SEO' factors" [2].

The rule of thumb behind the number is the one that matters most. To reach the top ten, Ahrefs says you will need about as many backlinks as the pages already there, "if not more" [1]. Matching them is no promise of the top result, though Ahrefs calls a place somewhere in the top ten "a very good chance" [1]. Ahrefs is also candid about the limit: every tool uses the same 0 to 100 scale but calculates it differently, and a metric that accurately predicts your chances "doesn't exist" [1].

How Semrush calculates KD%

Direct answer

Semrush's KD% measures the effort it would take to rank organically in Google's top ten for a keyword. It is based on the median number of referring domains to the ranking pages, the median ratio of followed to nofollowed links, the median Authority Score of the ranking domains and features of the results page itself [3]. Semrush sorts the scores into six bands.

KD%Semrush's label [3]
0 to 14Very easy
15 to 29Easy
30 to 49Possible
50 to 69Difficult
70 to 84Hard
85 to 100Very hard

The "authority" part deserves a caution. Google says: "some third-party services provide "reputation" or "authority" scores for sites. These don't correspond to any of Google's own signals nor come from Google." [4] Authority Score is Semrush's measure, useful for comparing sites inside Semrush, not a number Google uses.

What difficulty misses: your site, the map pack, AI answers

Direct answer

A difficulty score leaves out three things that decide real results. It ignores your own site, so the same score means different odds for different businesses [1]. It covers organic results only [2], so it says nothing about the map results, which Google says are mainly based on relevance, distance and popularity [5]. And it doesn't measure which pages Google's AI answers cite.

  • Your site. Ahrefs says the score shows how hard it is for the average website to rank, not yours [1]. A site with strong links and relevant pages can take on phrases a new site can't.
  • The map pack. Google says local results are mainly based on relevance, distance and popularity, with links and reviews feeding prominence [5], and that the categories you choose affect local ranking [6]. None of those inputs is in a KD score.
  • AI answers. Google says AI Overviews often don't appear [7]. When they do, studies disagree on how often they cite top-ten pages: 16.7% of citations (BrightEdge, September 2025) [8], 76% of cited pages (Ahrefs, July 2025) [9] and about 38% (Ahrefs, March 2026) [10].
  • Volume. Difficulty is read next to search volume, and both are estimates. Google Ads averages volume over 12 months by default, groups close variants and rounds the figures [11]. Most keywords are small: almost 93% of those in Ahrefs' US database get fewer than 10 searches a month [12].

Key takeaway

Keyword difficulty measures one list, the organic top ten, for an average site; a local business competes in more places than that.

Looking past the average: the weakest page on page one

Direct answer

A difficulty score is an average, and averages hide weak spots. Ahrefs itself says KD scores can hide specific weaknesses in the results that you can exploit [1]. So open page one and look at each result: its referring domains, how well it matches the searcher's intent and how complete it is. The weakest page there, not the average, is the one you have to pass.

To reach page one you don't have to beat the leader; you have to beat the last result on the page. For each ranking page, record:

  1. Intent and format. Is it a service page, a guide, a directory, a list? Match what Google already shows.
  2. Topics covered. Google says it looks for "other relevant content beyond the keyword" [13], so list the subtopics each result covers.
  3. Referring domains to that page. Count the sites linking to the ranking URL itself, the input both tools use [1][3].
  4. Quality. Run it through Google's own helpful-content questions, such as whether it offers "insightful analysis or interesting information that is beyond the obvious" [14].

Links matter, but Google says "We have many ranking signals, and PageRank is just one of those." [16] A page that answers the search better can pass one with more links. This page-by-page look is the core of SEO competitor analysis and a content gap analysis.

Using difficulty to order the work

Direct answer

Use keyword difficulty to decide the order, not the outcome. Group your phrases by service, modifier and place, measure the weakest page-one holder for each, and start where the gap is smallest. Long-tail phrases usually come first: most keywords are low-volume [12], and Ahrefs found that high-volume keywords take longer to rank for [15].

Ahrefs' timing study is a useful baseline: only 1.74% of random new pages reached the top ten within a year, and high-volume keywords took longer [15]. Those were pages with no targeted work behind them, so the figure shows that page one is not automatic, not what a planned campaign will do. The plan that follows is simple: work the smallest gaps first, in waves, and let links and reviews build toward the harder phrases. Local SEO keyword research covers building the list, and how long SEO takes to work covers the timing.

Checklist: reading a keyword difficulty score

Frequently asked questions

What is a good keyword difficulty score?

It depends on your site, because the score describes the average site, not yours [1]. Semrush labels 0 to 14 very easy, 15 to 29 easy and 30 to 49 possible [3], a sensible range for a newer site to start in, while a well-linked site can aim higher. The better test is the weakest page on page one: if your page can beat it, the phrase is within reach.

Why do Ahrefs and Semrush show different difficulty scores?

Because they calculate it differently. Ahrefs builds KD from the referring domains of the top ten organic pages [2]. Semrush blends median referring domains, the followed-to-nofollowed ratio, median Authority Score and features of the results page [3]. Ahrefs itself says every tool uses the same 0 to 100 scale but calculates it its own way [1]. Compare scores within one tool, never across tools.

Does keyword difficulty apply to Google Maps?

No. Ahrefs' KD is built from organic results only [2], and the map results run on different inputs: Google says local results are mainly based on relevance, distance and popularity, with links and reviews feeding prominence [5], and that categories affect local ranking [6]. For map phrases, compare Business Profiles instead: categories, reviews, rating, recency and distance.

Can a new site rank for high-difficulty keywords?

Eventually, but rarely first. Ahrefs found that only 1.74% of random new pages reached the top ten within a year, and that high-volume keywords take longer to rank for [15]. A new site does better starting with long-tail variations whose page-one holders are weak, earning links and reviews as it goes, then moving up to the harder phrases.

How many backlinks do I need for a keyword?

Ahrefs' rule of thumb is roughly as many as the pages already in the top ten have, if not more [1]. Count referring domains, the separate sites linking, to each ranking page, and compare against the weakest one on page one rather than the average. Links are one signal among many [16], so content and intent still have to match the search.

Measure first, then promise

Keyword difficulty is a starting estimate; the real measurement is the page-one competition for each phrase, in each place.
