Local SEO Keyword Research: How to Find Every Phrase Your Customers Type

Build the phrase list as services times modifiers times places, read Google's search data without being fooled by it, check intent on the live results page, and turn the list into a page plan.

Local keyword research means listing every way a customer could search for what you do, where you do it: each service, times the modifiers people add, times the places you serve. Then you check each phrase against Google's own data and the live results page before you plan a single page.

The short answer

Direct answer

Build the list as services × modifiers × places, then check it against Google's own data: Keyword Planner, autocomplete, your Business Profile's search terms and Search Console's queries [1][2][3][4]. Expect most phrases to be small. In Ahrefs' US database, almost 93% of keywords get fewer than 10 searches a month [5]. That long tail is where a local business can compete.

The multiplication is the point. A local business rarely wins by chasing one big phrase. It wins by covering the many specific ways real customers ask for the same service, and by building pages good enough to earn a place for them. This article is the phrase-list part of our local SEO guide.

Start with services and sub-services

Direct answer

List every service you offer, then every sub-service and every name customers use for it. Your Google Business Profile is a good start: Google suggests services tailored to your business type, and a listed service may be highlighted on your profile when people search for it [6]. Add the plain words customers use, which often differ from trade terms.

A dental practice's list might open with "dentist", "emergency dentist", "dental implants" and "cosmetic dentist", then break each one down: implant types, same-day options, sedation, pediatric care. A home-services business works the same way: "garage door repair", then springs, openers, cables, new doors.

Keep each service as its own row, because each will probably need its own page. Whitespark's 2026 survey of local search experts ranks a dedicated page for each service first among local organic factors and second for AI search visibility [7].

Add modifiers: near me, best, cost, emergency, open now

Direct answer

Modifiers are the words customers add to a service: "near me", "best", "cost", "emergency", "open now", "for kids". Each signals a different need, so each can deserve different treatment on the page. Google has documented "near me" searches shifting toward specific things at specific times, with fast growth in searches like "near me now" in its 2015 to 2017 data [8].

Stat

Google reported 150%+ growth in US mobile searches for "___ near me now" and 900%+ for "___ near me today/tonight", comparing July to December 2015 with the same months of 2017 [8].

Those figures are old, and Google published them in 2018; use them as a sign of direction, not as current numbers. Google also treats a bare category search as a nearby search. Its own example: "if you search for coffee shops, you're likely searching for coffee shops near you" [9]. More on this in our guide to near me searches.

Add places: city, neighborhoods, nearby towns

Direct answer

Add every place customers name: your city, its neighborhoods, nearby towns and ZIP codes within the area you serve. Keyword Planner can suggest nearby locations and break results down by location [10]. Many searches name no place at all, and Google still localizes them from the searcher's location [9], so keep the place-free versions on your list too.

Industry shorthand calls a phrase with a place in it "explicit" and one without a place "implicit". Google's own statement that it shows nearby results even when you don't include a location is what makes the implicit ones count [9].

Match the list to the area you really serve. A service-area business can set up to 20 service areas on its Business Profile [11], and its phrase list should follow the same map. What you must not do is answer each place with a near-identical page. Google's spam policies name "multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page" as doorway abuse [12].

Read Google's search data correctly (close variants, rounding, blanks)

Direct answer

Keyword Planner's average monthly searches cover a keyword and its close variants, averaged over 12 months by default, for the location and network you set, and rounded [1]. Historical figures show only for exact matches [1]. So figures for variants don't add up, and a blank isn't proof that nobody searches: small phrases can show no figure at all.

Stat

Of 606 phrases sent to Google Ads on September 27, 2026, 448 returned a monthly figure and 158 (26%) returned none [13].

The same pull showed how close variants behave. Six versions of one phrase, including "seo competitor analysis", "competitor analysis seo" and "seo competitor research", each returned 1,300 [13]. That is one bucket reported six times, not six separate audiences, so never add variant figures together.

Two lists come from searches that already reach you. Your Business Profile's Performance report lists the search terms people used to find your business, updated at the start of each month and including activity from Google Ads [3]. Search Console's Performance report groups your data by the query users typed [4]. Autocomplete adds a third view: its predictions reflect real searches that have been done on Google, and vary with the location a query comes from [2].

Check intent on the results page

Direct answer

Search each phrase from your market and look at what Google shows: a map pack, organic results, an AI Overview, ads. The layout tells you the intent and which place you can win. In a September 2026 check of 12 commercial phrases searched from Las Vegas, the map pack showed for 11 and an AI Overview for only 2 [14]; informational phrases showed them far more often [15].

In that second check, all 36 informational phrases searched nationally showed an AI Overview, and only one showed a local pack [15]. Commercial and informational phrases live on different pages of Google, and they need different pages on your site.

Search from the right place. Google says its results depend on many signals, including the words of your query and your location and settings [16], so a check from your office or a national setting can mislead. Sistrix makes the commercial point: the intent of a keyword defines the layout of the results page, and with it how many organic clicks are on offer [17].

A practical sort: "emergency dentist near me" wants the map results and a service page; "dental implants cost" wants a clear pricing section or page; "how long do dental implants last" wants an article.

From list to plan: grouping phrases by page

Direct answer

Group phrases that share a service and an intent onto one strong page, give each real service and real location its own page, and never build a near-identical page per variation. Google's spam policies call pages created to rank for specific, similar search queries doorway abuse [12]. Then order the groups by the gap between your pages and the pages already on page one.

Difficulty scores estimate part of that gap. Ahrefs' Keyword Difficulty is built from the linking domains of the current top ten pages, on a scale of 0 to 100, and doesn't account for your own site's authority [18]. Ahrefs also found that high-volume keywords take longer to rank for than low-volume ones [19], one more reason the long tail comes first. The details are in our guides to keyword difficulty, content gap analysis and SEO competitor analysis.

This is how our own phrase lists are built. Our written guarantee terms say we build a list of every variation your customers could type to find a business like yours, agree it with you in writing at the start, and leave out searches for your own business name. The reasoning behind that list is in how an SEO guarantee works, under the written terms.

Checklist: local keyword research

Frequently asked questions

How do I find local keywords?

List your services, add the modifiers customers use and the places you serve, then validate the list with Google's data: Keyword Planner [1], autocomplete [2], your Business Profile's search terms [3] and Search Console's queries [4]. Finally, search each phrase from your market to see what Google shows. The result is a phrase list built from real searches, not guesses.

Should I target keywords that show zero searches?

Often, yes, if they name a real service in a place you serve. Keyword Planner rounds its figures and groups close variants [1], and small phrases can show no figure at all: in our September 2026 pull, 26% of 606 phrases returned none [13]. Almost 93% of keywords in Ahrefs' US database get fewer than 10 searches a month [5]. Check the results page before you drop any phrase.

Should I add my city to every keyword?

No. Google shows nearby results even when a search includes no location [9], so place-free phrases like "emergency dentist" belong on the list alongside the versions with your city in them. Keep both forms on your list. On your pages, name your city where it reads naturally, and never build near-identical city pages just to hold place names; Google calls that doorway abuse [12].

What are keyword modifiers?

Modifiers are the words people add to a service to say what they need: "near me", "best", "cost", "emergency", "open now", "for kids". Each one changes the intent, and often the page that should answer it. Google's data from 2015 to 2017 showed fast growth in "near me now" and "near me today/tonight" searches on mobile [8], a sign that timing words matter.

How many keywords should a local business target?

As many real variations as your services, modifiers and places produce, minus your own business name. A practice with 10 services, 5 modifiers and 4 places has 200 combinations before pruning; that is arithmetic, not a target. Prune phrases that don't match a real service or place, then group the rest by page. Most will be small, long-tail phrases [5].
