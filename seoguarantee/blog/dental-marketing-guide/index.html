Dental Marketing Strategies: A Complete Guide for Practices

The dental marketing channels that work together, in the order to build them: local search, reviews, paid search, social, email and referrals, with the budget signals the data supports, tracking that keeps patient data out of ad platforms, and the ADA's fee-splitting line.

The dental marketing strategies that work start where patients start: Google search, the map, reviews and, more and more, AI answers. Build those first, measure them without sending patient information to ad platforms, then use paid search, social media, email and referrals to fill the gaps. This guide covers each channel in that order, the budget signals the data actually supports, tracking after the 2024 court ruling on HHS's tracking guidance, and the ADA Code's line on paying marketers a share of your fees.

How patients find a dentist today

Direct answer

Many patients now find a dentist the way they find any local business: they search Google, look at the map, read reviews and, increasingly, ask an AI tool. In Tebra's 2025 survey of 3,964 US adults, 56% started their search for a healthcare provider on Google and 79% read reviews before choosing one [1]. Your marketing has to show up, and look trustworthy, at each step.

The market you are marketing into, in the ADA Health Policy Institute's and federal figures:

MeasureFigure
Dentists in the US202,485 in 2024; 205,088 professionally active as of 2025 [2][3]
Dental office establishmentsAbout 136,000 [4][5]
National dental spending$189 billion in 2024, up 4% on 2023 [6]
People who visited a dentistAbout 45% of the population in 2022 [7]
Cost as a barrier13% reported cost barriers to dental care in 2023, against 4-5% for other health care [7]
Dentists "not busy enough"Around a quarter in Q2 2026 [8]
Wait for a new-patient visit13.9 days on average in Q2 2026 [8]

Two things in that table shape a dental marketing plan. Demand is not the problem everywhere: in the Q2 2026 panel, about a quarter of dentists said they were not busy enough, yet the average new patient waited 13.9 days [8]. The panel drew 589 responses from 2,432 invited dentists and leans toward ADA members, general practitioners and owners [8]. Know which kind of practice you are before you spend. And cost stops more people from getting dental care than any other kind of care [7], so clear cost, insurance and financing information is marketing, not an afterthought.

Competition is consolidating. The share of dentists affiliated with a dental support organization more than doubled from 7.2% in 2015 to 16.1% in 2024, and reached 26.5% among dentists within ten years of dental school [2]. In a consolidating market, knowing exactly who holds page one in your area matters more, not less.

Patients read before they book. In Tebra's research on healthcare reviews, 62% of patients had avoided booking with a provider because of negative reviews, and Google reviews ranked highest in influence, at 83% [9]. Consumers now ask AI tools too: in BrightLocal's 2026 survey, 45% of consumers had used AI for local business recommendations, up from 6% a year earlier, and 88% of AI users said they check what the AI cites [10].

Build the plan: capacity, budget and tracking

Direct answer

Start from capacity, not a budget percentage: how many new patients the practice can take each month, for which treatments, and what each is worth. No independent source gives a dental marketing budget benchmark we would stand behind, so set spend from your own numbers and the cost signals below, and track every lead from the first day.

A dental marketing plan answers four questions in writing:

  1. Capacity. How many new patients a month can you see, and in which chairs and hours?
  2. Treatments. Which treatments do you want more of, and what is a new patient for each worth to the practice?
  3. Market. Which places do your patients come from, and which practices already show up there?
  4. Measurement. What counts as a lead (a call, a form, an online booking), and where is each one recorded?

The cost signals worth knowing, each from a named source:

  • Paid search. LocaliQ's median cost for general dentistry search ads was $7.03 per click and $84.77 per lead, across 3,542 US campaigns from October 1, 2024 to September 30, 2025 [11]. A simple way to use it: at that median, ten leads a month cost about $850 in clicks before any management fee.
  • Practice economics. The ADA Health Policy Institute reports that over five years, supply prices and staff wages rose 23% while reimbursement rose 19% [8], and that inflation-adjusted general-practice income fell from $267,168 in 2010 to $207,980 in 2024 dollars [2]. Every marketing dollar has to earn its place.
  • Patient spending. Consumer spending on dental care was up only 1% over the 12 months to Q2 2026 [8].
  • Paid space on the page. Sterling Sky saw local pack ads on almost 22% of its reports, up from 1%, and Local Services Ads on 31% of the queries it tracked, up from 11%, as paid placements grew through 2025 [12]. More of the page is for sale than before.

Then set up tracking before the first campaign. A verified Business Profile's Performance report lists the search terms that found you and includes activity from both unpaid results and Google Ads [13]. Search Console's "queries" dimension shows the searches that bring up your pages [14]. ChatGPT tags its referrals with utm_source=chatgpt.com [15]. What to avoid is feeding patient information to ad platforms along the way; the HIPAA section below covers what HHS says about it.

Paying for marketing: the ADA's fee-splitting line

Direct answer

How you pay a marketer matters as much as how much. The ADA Code says a dentist who pays a marketing vendor a specific amount or percentage of the professional fees collected from actual or prospective patients "is engaged in fee splitting". The Code binds ADA members. In our reading, that puts per-patient and revenue-share contracts in question, so have any such contract reviewed before you sign [16].

The line sits at section 4.E.1 of the Code's 2025 edition, whose official advisory opinions were revised to October 2024 [16]. It speaks of fees collected "from actual or prospective patients" and of vendors "providing advertising or marketing services" [16]. Our reading, not the Code's text: per-patient lead deals and revenue-share SEO or ads contracts are the arrangements that raise the question.

Three questions to ask any marketing vendor before you sign:

  • How is your fee calculated, and does any part of it depend on patients, treatments or fees collected?
  • Who owns the Business Profile, the website, the ad accounts and the data if we part ways?
  • What exactly is promised, by when, and how will it be measured?

Put the answers in front of your counsel or your state board. And keep one more distinction in mind: a promise about search visibility is not a promise about patients. Our first page guarantee never promises patients, calls or bookings.

Local SEO and the Business Profile

Direct answer

Local search is where many patients start, and appearing in it can't be bought: Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking [17]. The work is a complete Business Profile with the right primary category, a page on your website for each treatment, a steady flow of reviews and consistent listings.

Google says local results are "mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity" [17]. In our own check of five dental searches made from Las Vegas on September 27, 2026, every one showed a map pack of practices above the organic results [18]. Experts weight the map pack mostly toward the Business Profile and reviews: in Whitespark's 2026 survey of 47 local search experts, profile signals carried 32% of the map-pack weight and reviews 20% [19].

The essentials, in short:

  • The primary category. It was the highest-scoring map-pack factor in Whitespark's survey [19], and Google says the categories you select affect your local ranking [20]. For most general practices that is Dentist; PlePer's 2026 US list confirms Dentist, Dental clinic, Cosmetic dentist, Emergency dental service and the specialist categories [21].
  • Services. List every treatment as a service with a description; Google says a listed service may be highlighted when someone searches for it [22].
  • Treatment pages. A dedicated page for each service was the strongest local organic factor in Whitespark's survey [19].
  • Completeness. Google says complete and accurate profiles are more likely to show up [17], and it lists an up-to-date Business Profile among the basics for its AI features [23].

Reviews and reputation

Direct answer

Reviews are marketing your patients write. Ask every patient for an honest Google review with a link or QR code, reply to every review without confirming that anyone is a patient, and never pay, discount or filter. In Tebra's research, 62% of patients had avoided booking a provider because of negative reviews, and Google reviews carried the most influence of any source [9].

What patients and consumers look for:

Stat

In BrightLocal's 2026 survey of 1,002 US adults, 74% of consumers only cared about reviews from the last three months, 68% would only use a business with four or more stars, and 80% said they were likely to use a business that responds to all its reviews [24].

Healthcare readers are just as demanding. Press Ganey found people read 4.7 reviews on average before choosing a provider, and most treat 4 stars as the lowest acceptable rating [25]. In Tebra's 2025 survey, seven in ten patients said a provider's reply to a negative review improved their opinion [1].

The rules for asking are strict, and they come from three places:

  • Google bans incentives of any kind for reviews, asking only happy customers, pressure to review while patients are in the office, and requests for specific content or staff names [26]. A review link or QR code is fine [27].
  • The FTC's rule, in force since October 21, 2024, bans businesses from fake reviews, incentives tied to a particular sentiment, undisclosed reviews by their own managers and review suppression through threats [28][29].
  • The ADA says reviews should not be posted by anyone employed by the practice, recommends strongly against paying for reviews, and notes that some jurisdictions may prohibit soliciting reviews from patients [30].

Replies carry legal risk for a dental practice. HHS has penalized dental practices from $10,000 to $62,500 over disclosures of patient information, in review replies and elsewhere [31][32], and after one settlement over review replies OCR's director put it bluntly: "This is a clear NO." [33] The ADA's model reply stays in broad "all patient" terms and never references a specific person or incident [34].

A review routine that stays inside all three rulebooks:

  1. Ask every patient at the same point in the visit, usually checkout.
  2. Hand over a card with the Google review link or QR code.
  3. Reply to every review within a few days, in general terms.
  4. Take complaints offline, and fix the cause.
  5. Never offer anything in return, never filter, never script.

Paid search and Local Services Ads

Direct answer

Paid search buys a place in an auction, so it can bring leads from the first week and stops when the budget does. LocaliQ's medians for general dentistry search ads, across 3,542 US campaigns from October 2024 to September 2025, were $7.03 per click and $84.77 per lead. Health ads face extra Google Ads limits, and no ad spend improves your unpaid map ranking [11][35][17].

LocaliQ's dental benchmarks, as medians [11]:

These are LocaliQ's own client campaigns, so treat them as a reference point, not a forecast for your market.

Google's health policies shape dental ads. Health is a sensitive category in Google Ads that includes "Invasive medical procedures, including cosmetic surgery, surgical procedures, or injections", and in that category "You can't use advertiser-curated audiences", which include Customer Match and your own data segments [35]. We found nothing in Google's policy saying whether implant or oral-surgery ads count as surgical procedures, so check how your own ads are classified before you plan remarketing to patient lists.

Ads and unpaid rankings are separate. Google says there is no way to pay for a better local ranking [17], and Whitespark's experts rate taking part in Google Ads a myth as a map ranking factor [19]. Our SEO vs PPC article compares the two for dentists.

Local Services Ads changed in 2025. Google replaced its old Google Guaranteed badge, never a first page ranking promise, with a single Google Verified badge on October 20, 2025, along with its other Local Services Ads badges [36][37]. Google also ended the reimbursement program tied to the old badge, keeping only services booked before December 7, 2025 eligible [38]. Local Services Ads are ranked in an auction on bid and profile quality, which includes your rating, number of reviews, response time, images and completed verification checks [39], and Google's screening varies by business category and location [40]. Check whether they are offered for your category where you practise. Our article on Google Guaranteed explains the change and how the badge differs from a first page SEO promise.

Social media, email and referral programs

Direct answer

Social media, email and referrals mostly work on people who already know you: current patients, their families and their neighbors. They are also where the ADA Code, the FTC and HIPAA meet. Disclose paid endorsements, keep before-and-after claims honest, and get counsel's sign-off before patient information, photos or stories appear in marketing [16][41][42].

Dental social media marketing. The ADA Code's 2025 wording says no dentist shall advertise in a way that is false or misleading in any material respect, "including via social media" [16]. The Code's 2025 update, announced on February 24, 2025, also says anyone endorsing a product or service must disclose paid partnerships, and that unsupported safety, efficacy or superiority claims are misleading whether they appear in print, on social media or through a dental influencer [16][43]. The FTC's rule separately bans fake indicators of social media influence [28].

Before-and-after photos. The FTC's Endorsement Guides give the example of an ad that simply features "before" and "after" pictures: it is likely to convey that the result shown is what people will generally achieve [41]. HIPAA's Privacy Rule lists full-face photographs among its identifiers [44], so treat smile makeovers with care and let counsel decide what authorization you need.

Email and patient lists. HIPAA defines marketing as a communication about a product or service that encourages people to buy or use it, and the rule requires a patient's authorization before their information is used for marketing, with narrow exceptions such as a "face-to-face communication" or a "promotional gift of nominal value" [42]. Where a third party pays for the communication, the authorization must say so [42]. Recall reminders, newsletters and offers to existing patients are exactly where those lines get tested; have counsel draw them for your practice.

Offers and free exams. Some states regulate promotional offers. In Florida, an advertisement for a free or discounted service by a dentist, chiropractor, physician or other listed practitioner must carry a statement that the patient has the right to refuse to pay, cancel payment or be reimbursed for any other service performed as a result of, and within 72 hours of, responding to the advertisement [45]. Check your own state's rules before you advertise a free exam.

A dental referral program. Referrals from happy patients are among the best dental marketing ideas there is, and the simplest version needs no incentive: ask, thank, and make referring easy. Before you offer anything of value for a referral, check with counsel or your state board. And keep review requests separate: Google bans any incentive for a review [26].

Posts on your Business Profile. Google Business Profile posts are a lightweight channel for updates, offers and events; posts older than six months are archived unless a date range is set [46]. Whitespark's experts rate the number of posts a ranking myth [19], so post for patients, not for rankings.

HIPAA-safe tracking after AHA v. Becerra

Direct answer

In June 2024 a federal court in Texas vacated one part of HHS's guidance on tracking technologies: the position that linking an IP address to a visit to a public page about health conditions or providers triggers HIPAA. HHS did not appeal. The rest of the guidance still stands, including its lines on patient portals, booking pages, business associate agreements and cookie banners [47][48][49].

How the guidance got here:

DateWhat happened
December 2022HHS's Office for Civil Rights issues its bulletin on online tracking technologies [49]
July 20, 2023The FTC and HHS write to about 130 hospital systems and telehealth providers about tools such as the Meta pixel and Google Analytics [50]
March 18, 2024HHS revises the bulletin [49]
June 20, 2024In American Hospital Association v. Becerra, the Northern District of Texas vacates only the position on IP addresses and public pages [47]
August 29, 2024The AHA reports that HHS will not appeal [48]

The court said the vacatur was "not intended to" limit the rest of the guidance [47]. What the bulletin still says, in HHS's words where short enough to quote:

  • Tracking technologies on pages that patients log in to "generally have access to PHI" [49].
  • Pages that let people schedule appointments without logging in may have access to protected health information in certain circumstances, and then "the tracking technology vendor is a business associate, and a BAA is required" [49].
  • Cookie banners that ask users to accept or reject tracking "do not constitute a valid HIPAA authorization" [49].
  • Disclosing protected health information to tracking vendors for marketing without patients' authorizations would be impermissible [49].

For a dental practice, the "Book online" button and the new-patient forms are exactly these pages. HIPAA's rules bind covered entities and their business associates; whether and how they apply to your practice is a question for your counsel, and no tracking setup is "approved" in the abstract. Note too that the 2023 FTC and HHS letter went to hospital systems and telehealth providers, not to dentists [50].

What a careful setup looks like, to review with counsel:

  1. List every script and pixel on your booking, forms and portal pages.
  2. Ask each vendor whether it will sign a business associate agreement.
  3. Keep ad pixels off those pages unless counsel has cleared the setup.
  4. Measure with sources that report totals, such as the Performance report and Search Console.
  5. Post your notice of privacy practices on the website; the Privacy Rule says a covered entity with a website must post it there prominently [44].

Our article on HIPAA marketing rules goes further, and dental website design covers the rest of the site.

A 90-day dental marketing plan

Direct answer

Spend the first 30 days on the foundation: tracking, the Business Profile and pages for your most valuable treatments. Use days 31 to 60 for reviews, listings and a first paid search campaign on those treatments. Use days 61 to 90 to measure, cut what isn't working and plan the next quarter. The order matters more than the budget.

DaysFocusActions
1-30FoundationTracking and the tag review with counsel; Business Profile category, services, hours and photos; pages for your top three treatments
31-60Proof and reachA review request for every patient; name, address and phone consistent across listings; a paid search campaign on one or two treatments
61-90Measure and adjustCost per lead by channel; search terms from the Performance report and Search Console; the next three treatment pages

Frequently asked questions

How much should a dental practice spend on marketing?

No independent source we trust gives a standard percentage, so work from your numbers: new patients needed each month, what each is worth, and the cost per lead in your market. For paid search, LocaliQ's 2024-25 medians for general dentistry were $7.03 per click and $84.77 per lead [11]. Local SEO and reviews cost time and fees rather than clicks, and Google says no one can pay for a better local ranking [17].

What is the best marketing for a new dental practice?

Start with what patients see first: a complete Google Business Profile with the right primary category, a website with a page for each treatment you want to grow, and honest reviews from your first patients. In Whitespark's 2026 survey, the primary category and a dedicated page for each service were the strongest map and local organic factors [19]. Add paid search for your priority treatments while those build.

Is SEO or Google Ads better for dentists?

They do different jobs. Google Ads buys a place in an auction and stops when the budget does; LocaliQ's median general dentistry cost was $84.77 per lead [11]. SEO earns the map and organic results, which Google says can't be bought [17][51]. Ads can fill gaps while SEO builds, and our SEO vs PPC article compares the two in detail.

How do dentists get more reviews?

Ask every patient, in person, with a direct Google review link or QR code, which Google allows [27]. Don't offer discounts or gifts, ask only happy patients or script the wording: Google bans all three [26]. The ADA adds that reviews shouldn't be posted by anyone employed by the practice [30]. A steady flow from real patients beats a burst [19].

What does HIPAA say about dental marketing?

HIPAA's Privacy Rule defines marketing as a communication encouraging people to buy or use a product or service, and requires a patient's authorization before their information is used for it, with narrow exceptions such as face-to-face communications [42]. HHS's tracking bulletin adds that where a tracking vendor has access to patient information, for example on a booking page, the vendor is a business associate and a BAA is required [49]. How that applies to your practice is a question for counsel.

Can a practice pay Google to rank higher on the map?

No. Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking [17], and that it never accepts money to include or rank sites in its organic results [51]. Local Services Ads and search ads are paid placements, ordered in auctions [39]. Whitespark's experts also rate taking part in Google Ads a myth as a map ranking factor [19].

A plan you can measure

Every channel above works better when it is measured against the same phrase list and the same competitors. Our first page guarantee is offered after an assessment: at least 20% of the agreed phrases on the first page by day 90, 40% by day 180 and 50% by the end of the first year, measured separately in each of the three places, which are the map results, the organic results and Google's AI answers. It is not a promise of patients, calls or bookings. A free SEO audit shows where your practice stands, and the Katy Dental Experts case study shows one practice on our results hub.
