SEO Guarantee Red Flags: How to Spot an SEO Scam

Ten red flags drawn from Google's, the FTC's and Ahrefs' warnings: promised positions, fake Google ties, bought links, fake reviews and first page promises with no written terms.

You spot an SEO scam by what it promises and how it plans to deliver: a fixed ranking, a claimed tie to Google, an uninvited pitch, secret methods, bought links, fake reviews or a promise on phrases nobody searches. Each red flag below is sourced to Google's own guidance, the FTC or Ahrefs.

The short answer: ten red flags

Direct answer

A pitch is a red flag when it (1) promises the top result or any fixed position, (2) claims special access to Google, (3) arrives uninvited, (4) won't explain its methods, (5) asks you to link to them, (6) relies on bought links, (7) builds doorway pages, (8) offers fake reviews, (9) stuffs keywords into your profile name or (10) makes a first page promise on phrases nobody searches.

Flags one to five come from Google's advice on hiring an SEO [1]. Six to nine break Google's rules [4][7][8], and fake reviews can break an FTC rule too [5]. Ten comes from Ahrefs, an SEO tool vendor [9].

Promised positions and "special" Google access (Google's own warning)

Direct answer

Google's hiring advice says no one can promise the top ranking on Google, and warns against SEOs who promise rankings, claim a special tie to Google or sell a paid fast lane into it [1]. Google also says it never accepts money to include or rank sites in its results [1]. A pitch built on a position or a Google connection is selling something Google says does not exist.

That sentence is why a fair first page guarantee promises no position at all, and it is where the longer answer to can SEO be guaranteed starts. Google adds that "An SEO audit should be about giving you realistic estimates of improvement, and an estimate of the work involved." [1] It tells owners to find someone else if an SEO promises the top result [1]. Google's John Mueller, on Google's Search Off The Record podcast as reported by Search Engine Journal, called promises about rankings or traffic "usually a red flag", since nobody outside Google can reach into its systems and change the rankings [2].

On money, Google is just as direct: "Google never accepts money to include or rank sites in our search results, and it costs nothing to appear in our organic search results." [1] For the map results, Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking [10]. Pitches to get you "Google Guaranteed" are out of date as well: Google replaced that ads badge in October 2025 [16], and it was never a first page promise.

Callers claiming to be Google (the FTC case)

Direct answer

In 2018 the FTC charged a Florida-based robocall scheme that falsely claimed to represent Google, falsely threatened businesses with removal from Google's results and falsely promised first page placement [3]. Treat any caller who sells rankings in Google's name as a scam: Google says it never takes money to rank sites [1]. Google's own automated calls concern customer requests, not rankings [11].

According to the FTC, the scheme then pitched a second program it falsely said could secure high placement in search results, for a one-time payment of $949.99 plus monthly payments of $169.99 or $99.99 [3]. Watch for Google listing scam calls that follow that script. Google does make some calls: it says it may contact a business with an automated call on a customer's behalf to complete a request, and owners can opt out [11].

Bought links, link schemes and doorway pages

Direct answer

Google's spam policies call links made mainly to manipulate rankings link spam, and name buying or selling links for ranking purposes as an example [4]. Pages made to rank for many similar searches, such as near-identical pages for each nearby city, are doorway abuse. Google says sites that break its spam policies may rank lower or not appear in results at all [4].

In Google's words:

  • "Link spam is the practice of creating links to or from a site primarily for the purpose of manipulating search rankings." [4]
  • "Doorway abuse is when sites or pages are created to rank for specific, similar search queries." [4]
  • "Scaled content abuse is when many pages are generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users." [4]

Paid links are allowed only when marked with a rel="nofollow" or rel="sponsored" attribute [4], and Google's spam policies now also cover attempts to manipulate the AI responses in Google Search [4]. On hiring, Google says: "You should never have to link to an SEO. Avoid SEOs that talk about link popularity schemes or submitting your site to thousands of search engines." [1] This is the core of black hat SEO, and Ahrefs notes that short-term results usually come from black hat tactics [9].

Fake reviews and keyword-stuffed profile names

Direct answer

Under the FTC's rule on consumer reviews, in force since October 21, 2024, it is an unfair or deceptive practice for a business to write, create or sell fake reviews, or to buy reviews about itself that it knew or should have known were fake [5][6]. Google's Maps policy goes further and bans paid reviews, review incentives and review gating [7]. Keyword-stuffed Business Profile names risk suspension [8].

Section 465.2 of the rule (16 CFR Part 465) covers reviews that misrepresent whether the reviewer exists, whether they used the product or service, or what their experience was [5]. When the FTC announced the rule on August 14, 2024, it said the rule also bans incentives conditioned on a review expressing a particular sentiment, positive or negative [12]. FTC staff confirm it took effect on October 21, 2024 [6]. The rule binds businesses.

Google's policy for Maps content lists paid reviews, incentives for posting reviews, and discouraging negative reviews or asking only happy customers as prohibited, and it bars staff review quotas [7]. Google says it blocked or removed more than 292 million policy-violating reviews and over 13 million fake Business Profiles in 2025 [13]. Fake Google reviews are a known target, not a quiet shortcut.

The profile name is the other trap. Google says a business name should reflect the real-world name, and that adding unnecessary information "could result in the suspension of your Business Profile" [8]. Whitespark's 2026 expert survey explains the temptation: keywords in the business title rank as a strong map factor, and keyword stuffing in the name ranks among the top suspension risks [14]. Do Google reviews help SEO and how to get Google reviews cover the honest route.

Keywords nobody searches, and your own brand name

Direct answer

A first page promise is only as good as the phrases it counts. Ahrefs lists the usual padding: keywords unrelated to your business, keywords with little or no search volume, and informational searches that will not bring leads [9]. Searches for your own business name are another: you should show up for those already, so a fair list leaves them out.

Ask to see the list before you sign, and read it the way a customer would. Under our written terms, the list covers every variation your customers could type to find a business like yours, it is agreed with you in writing at the start, and searches for your own business name do not count.

Long contracts tied to short first page guarantees

Direct answer

Ahrefs puts it plainly: "Don't sign a long-term contract on a short-term guarantee." [9] Watch for a short first page promise wrapped in a long contract. Check how long the promise runs, when and from where results are measured, and how you can leave. A fair first page guarantee spans its own milestones and puts the exit terms in writing.

What a fair first page guarantee looks like

Direct answer

A fair first page guarantee meets the standard the FTC's guides on advertising guarantees set: clear disclosure of its limits and conditions [15]. Its written terms name the phrases that count, where and from which location results are checked, on which dates, and what happens after a miss. It never promises a position or sales.

Most sections of those guides apply to "the advertising of any warranty or guarantee" and ask that a seller advertise one only if it performs it promptly and fully [15]. Here is what our first page guarantee sets out:

  • The promise. We guarantee up to 50% of the key phrases you could rank for will rank on the first page of Google.
  • The list. Every variation your customers could type, agreed in writing at the start; your own business name is excluded.
  • The three places. Your Google Business Profile in Google's local map results, page one of the organic results, and AI suggestions and answers (Google's own AI answer on the results page).
  • The schedule. At least 20% of the list on the first page within 90 days, 40% within 180 days and 50% by the end of the first year, measured separately in each of the three places (in AI answers, over the phrases for which Google shows one when checked).
  • The limits. No specific position, not every phrase, and no promise of traffic, calls, leads or sales.
  • Qualification. Offered after an assessment, and only if you qualify.

What happens if a milestone is missed is set out in the written guarantee terms, and how an SEO guarantee works explains the measurement behind the numbers.

Checklist: questions to ask any SEO before you sign

Frequently asked questions

Are SEO guarantees with written terms a scam?

Not by themselves. Google warns about promised rankings and the top result [1], and Ahrefs calls ranking guarantees over-confident at best [9]. A first page guarantee that promises no position, counts only agreed phrases, says when and from where it is checked, and puts its limits in writing is a different promise. The written terms are the test: read them before you sign.

What does a fake Google call sound like?

The FTC's 2018 charges say callers falsely claimed to represent Google, threatened to remove businesses from Google's results, and sold placement for a fee [3]. Google's own automated calls exist, but they concern a customer's request, and owners can opt out [11]. A call that sells rankings in Google's name is the pattern to hang up on.

Can an SEO company get a business special treatment from Google?

No. Google says it never accepts money to include or rank sites in its search results [1], and that there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking [10]. It warns against SEOs who claim special access, and says third-party SEO tools have no access to its internal ranking data [1]. An agency can do the work well; it cannot buy or arrange the result.

Is buying reviews illegal?

The FTC's rule on consumer reviews makes it an unfair or deceptive practice for a business to buy reviews about itself that it knew or should have known were fake, or to write, create or sell fake reviews [5]. It also bans incentives conditioned on a positive or negative review [12]. Google's Maps policy goes further and prohibits paid reviews and any incentive for a review [7].

What is black hat SEO?

Black hat SEO means tactics that break Google's rules to rank faster: bought links, link schemes, doorway pages and mass-produced pages [4], fake reviews [7] and keyword-stuffed profile names [8]. Google says sites that violate its spam policies may rank lower or not appear at all [4]. Ahrefs notes that short-term results usually come from black hat tactics [9], so a promise of fast rankings deserves a hard look.

What should an SEO contract include?

Answers to Google's own questions: what results to expect, in what timeframe, and how success will be measured [1]. If a guarantee is offered, the contract or its written terms should name the phrase list, the dates, where and from which market results are checked, what is not promised and what happens after a miss. It should also say how either side can end it.
