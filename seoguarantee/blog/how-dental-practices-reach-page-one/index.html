How Dental Practices Reach Page One: A Dental Local SEO Guide

Dental practices reach page one in three places, and each runs on different signals: the map pack, where your Google Business Profile competes; the organic results, where your treatment pages compete; and Google's AI answers, which draw on both. Google says local results are "mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity" [1], so the work is concrete: be the most relevant, best-reviewed practice within reach of the patient who is searching. This guide to local SEO for dentists takes each place in turn, then covers what the ADA's 2025 Code says about dental advertising.

What page one looks like for dental searches

Direct answer

On the dental searches we checked, page one had two parts and sometimes a third: a map pack of three practices drawn from Google Business Profiles, the organic results below it, and, for one of them, an AI Overview above both. The map pack runs on the profile, distance and reviews. The organic results run on your website. The AI answer draws on both.

We checked what that looks like in practice. On September 27, 2026, we searched five dental phrases from Las Vegas on desktop: "dentist", "emergency dentist", "dental implants", "cosmetic dentist" and "best dentist in las vegas" [2]. Every one showed a map pack and organic results. Only "dental implants" showed an AI Overview, and it linked dental practice websites outside Las Vegas rather than any practice in the map pack [2]. In that single capture, directories held some of the organic slots: Healthgrades and Zocdoc for "dentist", Yelp for "cosmetic dentist" [2]. One capture on one day proves no rule, but it shows the shape.

Patients start on this page. In Tebra's 2025 survey of 3,964 US adults, 56% said they begin their search for a healthcare provider on Google, and 79% read reviews before they choose one [3]. Location applies even when nobody types a place: Google says your location helps show nearby results "even if you didn't include a location in your search" [4]. A search for "dentist" is a local search.

What moves each place differs. Whitespark's 2026 survey asked 47 local search experts to weigh 187 factors. It measures expert opinion, not Google's code, and none of the experts has access to Google's local algorithm [5]. Their weights, by group of signals:

Signal groupMap packLocal organic resultsAI search visibility
Business Profile32%7%12%
Reviews20%6%16%
On-page (your website)15%33%24%
Links8%24%13%
Citations6%7%13%
Behavioural signals9%10%4%
Personalization6%8%9%
Social4%5%9%

Key takeaway

Treat the map pack, the organic results and the AI answer as three races with different rules: the profile and reviews lead the map, your pages and links lead the organic results, and AI answers draw on a mix of both.

The dental phrase list: treatments × modifiers × places

Direct answer

Build the list the way patients search: every treatment you offer, multiplied by the modifiers patients add, such as "near me", "emergency", "cost" or "open now", multiplied by the places you serve. Then check it against Google's own data: Keyword Planner, your Business Profile's search terms and Search Console. Most phrases are small, and many will show no volume at all.

Start with treatments, not with the word "dentist": cleanings, fillings, crowns, root canals, implants, dentures, whitening, veneers, clear aligners, emergency care and children's visits. Add the modifiers patients type, such as same day, open Saturday, for kids or "takes my insurance", then every place you draw patients from: the city, its neighborhoods, nearby towns and ZIP codes. Ten treatments, eight modifiers and six places already make 480 combinations before you prune.

Then prune. Remove searches for your own practice name, count each phrase once, and drop combinations nobody would type. What remains is the list you measure against the practices already on page one. If your practice is in Las Vegas or Houston, our pages on dental SEO in Las Vegas and dental SEO in Houston show the local picture.

Google's data is rough, so use several sources:

  • Keyword Planner. Volumes are rounded averages for a keyword and its close variants, over 12 months by default [6], and its "Nearby" option suggests places to add [7].
  • Your Business Profile. The Performance report lists the search terms people used to find you, updated at the start of each month [8].
  • Search Console. Its "queries" dimension shows the searches that already bring up your pages [9].
  • Autocomplete. Google says its predictions reflect real searches and vary by location [10].

Expect many phrases to show no volume at all. When we sent 606 phrases on SEO and marketing topics to Google Ads on September 27, 2026, 158 of them (26%) came back with no monthly figure, and close variants shared one number rather than adding up [11]. In Ahrefs' US database, almost 93% of keywords get fewer than 10 searches a month [12], and Ahrefs also found that high-volume keywords take longer to rank for than low-volume ones [13]. A dental list is mostly long tail, and the long tail is where measuring pays off.

Build phrases from what the practice is. The ADA Code asks general dentists to avoid communications that imply specialization (covered below) [14], so a general practice that offers clear aligners builds its phrases around the treatment, not around a specialist title.

The Business Profile: categories, services, photos and hours

Direct answer

Your Google Business Profile decides whether you appear in the map pack. Choose the one primary category that says what the practice is, add only the few extra categories that are also true, list every treatment as a service with a description, keep your hours exact and add real photos. Google says the categories you select affect your local ranking [15].

Start with the category. In Whitespark's 2026 survey, the primary Business Profile category was the highest-scoring map-pack factor of all, just ahead of the distance between the searcher and the business [5]. An incorrect primary category was the second-worst negative factor, behind only a listing marked permanently closed [5]. Google's rules are short:

  • You pick one primary category and can add up to 9 more; the category in the first field is the primary one [16].
  • Use the fewest categories that describe your core business, choose ones that complete the sentence "This business IS a", and never use categories as keywords [17].
  • You can't create your own category, and Google adds broader parent categories itself [15][17].
  • Adding or editing a category can trigger a new verification [15].

Google publishes no public list of categories. PlePer's 2026 US list confirms these for dental offices [18]:

Kind of practiceCategories in PlePer's 2026 US list
General practiceDentist; Dental clinic
Treatment-ledCosmetic dentist; Dental implants provider; Emergency dental service; Teeth whitening service; Denture care center
Specialist practiceEndodontist; Orthodontist; Oral surgeon; Oral and maxillofacial surgeon; Pediatric dentist; Periodontist; Prosthodontist; Dental implants periodontist
OtherDental hygienist; Dental radiology

For most general practices the right primary category is Dentist, because that is what the practice is. A general practice that places implants is still a dentist that offers implants, and the Services list is where implants belong. A specialist category belongs to a practice that is that specialty; Google's "IS a" test and the ADA Code's line on implying specialization point the same way.

Google counts dentists as individual practitioners, and a practitioner's profile may include a title such as Dr. [17]. For a solo dentist at a branded practice, Google's guideline is one shared profile named in the form "[brand]: [dentist's name]" [17]. Our article on Google Business Profile for dentists covers practitioner profiles in detail.

List every treatment as a service. Google lets businesses add custom services with a price and a description, suggests services by business type, and says a listed service may be highlighted on your profile when someone searches for it [19]. Predefined services also appear on Whitespark's list of map-pack factors [5].

Keep hours exact. Being open at the time of the search ranked fifth among Whitespark's 2026 map-pack factors [5]; Sterling Sky first reported the effect in December 2023, and Google has not confirmed it [20]. Google's guidelines ask for your regular customer-facing hours [17], the Whitespark report is blunt that you should never list hours other than your real ones [5], and special hours cover holidays and short closures [21].

Add real photos of the building, the team and the rooms patients see. Google says a photo should have no significant alterations or excessive use of filters or AI, because "the image should represent reality" [22]. Keep patient faces off the profile unless your counsel has approved it: HIPAA's Privacy Rule lists full-face photographs among its identifiers [23].

Complete everything you can. Google says businesses with complete and accurate information are more likely to show up in local results [1]. Two things you may read about are another matter: an "accepting new patients" attribute and a dental insurance picker. We found no Google source documenting either for dental profiles, so put new-patient and insurance details on your website, where you control them. Skip the myths: Whitespark's experts rate geotagged photos, keywords in review replies and the number of posts as myths, not ranking factors [5].

Checklist: the dental Business Profile

Treatment pages that answer patients' questions

Direct answer

Give every treatment you want to grow its own page on your website: what it is, who it suits, what the visit involves, recovery, cost factors and insurance, written or reviewed by a named dentist. In Whitespark's 2026 survey, a dedicated page for each service was the strongest local organic factor and the second strongest factor for AI search visibility [5].

The same factor placed only 24th for the map pack, which shows where these pages do their work: the organic results and AI answers [5]. The second-strongest local organic factor was geographic relevance, meaning the city and neighborhood words in your content [5].

The most basic relevance signal, Google says, is content that contains the same keywords as the search, and its systems then look for related content beyond the keyword [24]. For a page on dental implants, that means the questions around the treatment: bone grafts, healing time, how long implants last, cost factors and the alternatives.

Google also holds health pages to a higher bar. In its words: "our systems give even more weight to content that aligns with strong E-E-A-T for topics that could significantly impact the health, financial stability, or safety of people." [25] The same page says it helps people understand the E-E-A-T of content when it's clear who created it [25]. Put the dentist's name, credentials and a review date on every treatment page.

Each treatment page should answer:

  • What the treatment is, in plain words
  • Who it suits, and who it doesn't
  • What happens at each visit, and how long it takes
  • Recovery and aftercare
  • What affects the cost, and how insurance and financing work
  • Which dentist does it, with credentials, and how to book

Each city page needs its own content. Google's spam policies name "Having multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page" as doorway abuse [26].

Write claims you can back. The ADA Code lists statements "likely to create an unjustified expectation about results" among the examples of false or misleading advertising, along with unsupported claims that your services are superior to other dentists' [14]. "Painless", "best dentist in town" and promised clinical outcomes are the usual offenders.

The page also has to win the patient, not only the search. In Tebra's 2025 survey, 44% of respondents said a practice's website influences their decision [3]. Our guide to dental website design covers the rest of the site.

Reviews and replies without breaking patient privacy

Direct answer

Ask every patient for an honest Google review with a link or QR code, and never pay, discount, filter or script. Reply to reviews in general terms that never confirm the reviewer is a patient. Google's review policy, the FTC's 2024 rule and HIPAA enforcement all apply here, and HHS has penalized dental practices over what they posted in replies [27][28][29][35].

Reviews move the map. Google puts it plainly: "More reviews and positive ratings can help your business's local ranking." [1] Whitespark's experts give reviews 20% of the map-pack weight, and high ratings, the number of reviews with text and review recency all sit among the leading factors [5]. Sterling Sky and Places Scout studied 8,186 businesses in 200 US cities and found that, for "near me" rankings, the reviews a business gained in the current month mattered more than its total, and that reviews with text carried more weight than bare ratings [30].

Patients read them closely:

Stat

In Tebra's 2025 survey, 53% of adults said they won't consider a provider with fewer than 4 stars [3]. In Press Ganey's survey of 1,000 healthcare consumers, people read 4.7 reviews on average before choosing, and 64.8% called reviews more than a year old irrelevant [31].

Three sets of rules apply, and they don't match exactly:

  • Google's policy bans incentives such as payment, discounts, or free goods or services for any review, discouraging negative reviews or asking only happy customers, pressuring people to review while on your premises, and asking for specific content, including content that names a staff member [27]. Asking for honest reviews is fine, for example with a Google review link or a QR code [32].
  • The FTC's rule on consumer reviews took effect on October 21, 2024 [33]. It bans businesses from writing or buying fake reviews, from incentives tied to a particular sentiment, from undisclosed reviews by their own officers and managers, and from suppressing reviews with unfounded legal threats, physical threats or intimidation [28]. FTC staff say the rule doesn't reach incentives with no sentiment condition, and that asking only happy customers "could violate the FTC Act" [33]. Google's ban on all incentives still applies to every Google review.
  • The ADA's guidance says reviews should not be posted by anyone employed by the practice, strongly recommends against paying for reviews, and notes that "some jurisdictions may prohibit soliciting online reviews by patients" [34]. Check your state's rules before you build a request process.

Replies are where dental practices get hurt. HHS's Office for Civil Rights imposed a $50,000 civil money penalty on a North Carolina dental practice that disclosed a patient's information in a reply to a negative online review [29]. New Vision Dental in California paid $23,000 and agreed to a corrective action plan; OCR's director said providers cannot disclose patients' protected health information when responding to negative reviews, adding: "This is a clear NO." [35] Elite Dental Associates in Dallas agreed to pay $10,000 over social media disclosures [36].

The ADA's guidance shows the safe pattern: respond in very broad "all patient" terms, and "Never reference a specific person or incident." [37] Its sample reply begins: "Our office strives to provide the best service to all patients" [37]. Compare two replies of our own making. Risky: "Sorry your crown felt loose, Maria; we have checked your chart." Safer: "Thank you for the feedback; we want every patient to have a good visit, and we'd welcome a call from anyone who wants to talk about theirs."

Google checks replies before they post, which usually takes up to 10 minutes and sometimes up to 30 days, and reviewers can change their review after reading your reply [38]. Replies pay off: in Tebra's 2025 survey, seven in ten patients said a provider's reply to a negative review improved their opinion [3]. Our article on Google reviews and local rankings goes deeper on the ranking side, HIPAA marketing rules on the privacy side, and how to get more dental patients on what patients weigh when they choose.

Links and citations for dental practices

Direct answer

Links and citations are other websites vouching for your practice. Keep your name, address and phone number identical on your website, your Business Profile and the main directories, then earn links and mentions from local and dental sources. Google says prominence draws partly on how many websites link to your business, and Whitespark's experts rate citations a small map factor but a larger AI one [1][5].

Start with consistency. In Whitespark's survey, website contact details that match your Business Profile counted as a map-pack factor, a website with no crawlable name, address and phone placed among the ten worst negatives, and mismatched details or tracking phone numbers across the web also counted against a business [5].

Citations themselves are a modest map factor; the best-rated citation signal placed only 28th for the map pack [5]. The picture changes for AI. Whitespark's report says three of the top five AI search visibility factors are citation factors [5]. And in a small BrightLocal test of 20 searches across 10 industries and four AI tools, ChatGPT drew its dental answers only from dental directories, ten different ones [39].

Links still count. Google's How Search Works page names links from other prominent websites as one sign that information is trustworthy [24]. Ahrefs states the rule of thumb: to reach the first ten results for a phrase, acquire about as many backlinks as the pages already there, if not more [40]. That is the practical meaning of "do everything they did, and more".

Good dental link and mention sources: local press coverage of a new service or community event, your state and local dental society's member pages, the dental schools where your dentists trained, local sponsorships, and a claimed, correct Bing Places listing, which Microsoft points local businesses to for its AI answers [41].

Google's spam policies name "Buying or selling links for ranking purposes" as link spam [26]. For AI answers, Whitespark's leading visibility factors include appearing on expert-curated "best of" lists and being mentioned in newspaper articles, blog posts, government sites and industry associations [5], and Google's AI guide says "seeking inauthentic "mentions" across the web isn't as helpful as it might seem." [42]

AI answers: what gets a practice named or cited

Direct answer

Google says its AI features rest on its core ranking systems, need no special markup or files, and can include local businesses, with Business Profile information helping. So the work that wins the map and the organic results is the work that feeds AI answers. Measure AI answers separately, though: Google shows them for only some searches, and what they cite can shift between runs [42][43][50].

Google launched AI Overviews to everyone in the US on May 14, 2024 [44], and AI Mode reached all US users on May 20, 2025 [45]. Google says there are no additional requirements or special optimizations for either, and no need for new machine-readable files, AI text files or special schema.org markup [43]; Google Search ignores llms.txt files [42]. Two lines from its guidance matter most for a practice:

  • "Where appropriate, generative AI responses can include product listings, product information, and information about local businesses." [42]
  • "Using products like Merchant Center (such as Merchant Center feeds) and Google Business Profiles can help your products and services to be visible in both AI responses and other Google Search results." [42]

How often does a dental search show an AI answer? Less than you might think. In our Las Vegas capture, one of five dental searches did [2], and Google says AI Overviews appear only when its systems judge them additive, so they "often don't trigger" [43]. On US mobile searches, Sterling Sky found AI local packs on about 7% of the keywords it tracks, surfacing about 32% as many businesses as the classic map pack [46].

Which pages get cited is contested. Studies of how often AI Overviews cite pages from the organic first ten disagree: BrightEdge put it at 16.7% in September 2025, Ahrefs at 76% in July 2025 and about 38% in March 2026 [47][48][49]. In AI Mode, SE Ranking found that when an answer linked to a Google property, 97.9% of those links pointed to Google Maps business profiles, though such links appeared in only 9.2% of responses [50]. Your profile is part of your AI presence.

Consumers use other assistants as well. In BrightLocal's 2026 survey, 45% of consumers had used AI tools for local business recommendations, up from 6% a year earlier; ChatGPT was the tool most used for business recommendations (31%), followed by Google's AI Mode (23%) [51]. To appear in ChatGPT search, a site has to allow OpenAI's OAI-SearchBot crawler [52].

What to do about it: rank the treatment pages, keep the Business Profile and Bing Places complete, let Googlebot, Bingbot and OAI-SearchBot crawl the site, and earn genuine reviews and mentions. Then measure. Search Console counts AI Overviews and AI Mode traffic within the "Web" search type [43], its generative AI report, launched June 3, 2026, shows impressions [53], and ChatGPT tags its referrals with utm_source=chatgpt.com [54]. Our guide to AI search optimization covers the wider picture.

What the ADA Code (2025) says about advertising and specialist claims

Direct answer

The ADA's Code of Professional Conduct binds ADA members. Its 2025 edition says any dentist may advertise, but not in a way that is false or misleading in any material respect, including on social media. It names websites and SEO, requires paid influencer partnerships to be disclosed, and says general dentists must not imply they are specialists [14].

The 2025 edition was announced on February 24, 2025, and carries official advisory opinions revised to October 2024 [14][55]. Five parts touch search marketing:

SectionWhat the Code says
5.FAny dentist may advertise, but no dentist shall advertise in a way that is "false or misleading in any material respect", in any form of communication, including via social media [14]
5.F.2Statements likely to create an unjustified expectation about results, and unsupported claims of superiority over other dentists, are examples of misleading advertising [14]
5.F.6Websites must be truthful, and SEO techniques "should comport with" the Code [14]
5.F.7Unsupported safety, efficacy or superiority claims are misleading in print, on social media or through an influencer, and paid partnerships must be disclosed [14][55]
5.H and 5.I"Specialist" covers the 12 recognized specialties; general dentists must avoid communications that "express or imply specialization" and state that their services are provided by general dentists [14]

In search terms, our reading is that this reaches every page, profile description, post, ad and Business Profile category. The 12 recognized specialties are dental anesthesiology, dental public health, endodontics, oral and maxillofacial pathology, oral and maxillofacial radiology, oral and maxillofacial surgery, oral medicine, orofacial pain, orthodontics and dentofacial orthopedics, pediatric dentistry, periodontics, and prosthodontics [14].

State rules can go further, and they bind dentists licensed in that state. Two examples:

  • Texas. The dental board's rule, 22 Tex. Admin. Code §108.54 (amended effective April 1, 2021), limits "specialist" advertising to areas recognized by a certifying board and accredited by the ADA's Commission on Dental Accreditation. It also puts the burden on the practice owner to avoid any inference that general practitioners are specialists [56].
  • Florida. Section 466.0282 of the Florida Statutes requires a capital-letter disclaimer when a dentist announces a practice area that is not a recognized specialty, saying the area is not recognized as a specialty by the American Dental Association or the Florida Board of Dentistry [57].

A federal layer sits on top. The FTC's Endorsement Guides, updated June 29, 2023, treat a patient review or testimonial used in advertising as an endorsement that will likely be read as representing what patients generally achieve; they are guidance under the FTC Act, not a rule [58].

None of this tells you what your own practice may say; that is a question for your counsel or your state board. It does show that dental SEO carries an ethics layer most businesses don't have. And a search promise is not a clinical promise: our first page guarantee is about where a practice shows up on Google, never about treatment outcomes.

Measuring the practices already on page one

Direct answer

Reaching page one is a measurement problem before it is a marketing one. For every phrase on your list, record which practices hold the map pack, the organic results and any AI answer from inside your market, measure what they have, then match it and add more. That is our method for setting a first page share, not a statistic about outcomes.

What we record for each business already on page one:

PlaceWhat we measure
Map packPrimary and additional categories, distance from the search point, review count, rating and reviews in the last 90 days, profile completeness, and the business name
Organic resultsThe ranking page's format and depth, the treatments and questions it covers, the referring domains pointing to it, and page experience
AI answerWhether one shows at all for the phrase, and which pages and profiles it names or links

Then comes the arithmetic. For each phrase and each place, measure the gap between your practice and the weakest business holding a page-one slot there, then sort the phrases from the smallest gap to the largest. That order is the work plan. It is also what makes a written promise possible: the share we commit to is set from the measured gaps, with margin built into the schedule. This is a method, not a measured outcome, and Google still decides every result. Every tool estimates Google from the outside; Google says no third-party tool has access to its internal ranking data [59].

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take a dental practice to reach page one?

Google's starter guide says some changes take effect in a few hours and others take several months [60]. Without targeted work it is slow: only 1.74% of the new pages Ahrefs sampled reached the first ten results within a year [13]. Our first page guarantee sets dated milestones: at least 20% of the agreed list by day 90, 40% by day 180 and 50%+ by the end of the first year, in each of the three places.

What matters most for dental map rankings?

Google names relevance, distance and popularity, and says reviews and links feed the third [1]. In Whitespark's 2026 expert survey, the primary Business Profile category scored highest of all map-pack factors, just ahead of the distance to the searcher [5]. You can't move your building. You can choose the right category, complete the profile, keep reviews coming steadily and keep your hours exact.

Should each treatment have its own page?

Yes, for every treatment you want to grow. A dedicated page for each service was the strongest local organic factor and the second strongest AI visibility factor in Whitespark's 2026 survey [5]. Make each page useful: what the treatment is, who it suits, the visit, recovery and cost factors, with the dentist named. Each city page needs its own content too: Google's spam policies treat pages targeted at specific cities that funnel users to one page as doorway abuse [26].

Can a dentist reply to patient reviews?

Google lets owners reply to reviews [38], and patients notice: in Tebra's 2025 survey of 803 patients, 71% said they trust providers more when they respond to negative reviews [61]. The risk is what the reply says. HHS has penalized dental practices whose replies disclosed patient information [29][35]. The ADA's model reply stays in broad "all patient" terms and never mentions a specific person or incident [37].

Do dental practices appear in AI Overviews?

For some searches, not all. Google says AI Overviews appear only when its systems judge them additive, and that they often don't trigger [43]. In our Las Vegas check on September 27, 2026, only "dental implants" of five dental searches showed one, and it linked dental practice websites rather than the practices in the map pack [2]. Google's guidance points to strong pages and an up-to-date Business Profile [42].

How many reviews does a dental practice need?

Google publishes no number. Benchmark against the practices already in your map pack: their review count, their rating and how many reviews they gained in the last 90 days. Consumers set their own floors: in BrightLocal's 2026 survey, 47% of consumers said they won't use a business with fewer than 20 reviews, and 74% look for reviews written in the last three months [62]. A steady flow beats a burst [5].
